Current Electricity: JEE Main Physics formulas and notes

Current Electricity is one of the most scoring chapters of Class 12 and usually gives 2 questions in JEE Mains: a network of resistors, a cell or grouping question, Kirchhoff’s laws, a meter bridge or potentiometer, the conversion of a galvanometer, and heating or an RC circuit. Most questions are short calculations that depend on drawing the circuit properly and on a few standard results. About 8 hours spread over 6 days is enough.

Quick revision

  • I = nAvd e; J = I/A = σE; vd = μE = eEτ/m; σ = ne²τ/m = neμ
  • R = ρl/A; stretched n times: R′ = n²R; R = R₀(1 + αΔT); metals α > 0, semiconductors α < 0
  • Series: ΣR, V divides as R. Parallel: Σ1/R, I divides as 1/R
  • Cube: 7R/12, 3R/4, 5R/6; infinite ladder x² − ax − ab = 0; ring x(1 − x)R
  • V = E − Ir; series nE, nr; parallel E, r/m; mixed best when R = nr/m
  • P(load) = E²R/(R + r)²; maximum E²/4r at R = r, efficiency 50 %
  • KCL: sum of currents at a node = 0; KVL: sum of potential changes in a loop = 0
  • Bridge P/Q = R/S; meter bridge X = R l/(100 − l) (X on the left); r = R(l₁ − l₂)/l₂
  • Shunt S = Ig G/(I − Ig); series R = V/Ig − G; P = VI = I²R = V²/R; H = I²Rt; τ = RC

Formulas with solved examples

Current, drift and conduction

Current, charge and current density

  • I = (dq)/(dt)
  • charge through a section q = ∫I dt = area under the I–t graph
  • I = nAvd e (n free electrons per m³, area A, drift speed vd)
  • current density J = (I)/(A) = nevd (vector, along the direction of E for the conventional current)
  • I = ∫J·dA
  • conventional current is opposite to the electron flow
  • current is a scalar and it is the same at every section of a series wire (conservation of charge)

How to apply. Find q from I(t) by integrating, or read I from q(t) by differentiating. For a wire use I = nAvd e; for a non-uniform wire the current is the same everywhere but J and vd change with A.

Example. I = 3t² A: charge in the first 2 s is q = ∫3t² dt = t³ = 8 C. A current of 5 A in a wire of cross-section 2 mm² gives J = 5 ÷ (2 × 10⁻⁶) = 2.5 × 10⁶ A/m².

Current due to rotating charges

  • A ring or particle carrying charge q and making f turns per second: I = qf = (qω)/(2π) = (q)/(T)
  • for an electron in a Bohr orbit I = e f
  • the loop has magnetic moment M = IA (see Magnetism)
  • for a charged ring with surface or line charge use the total charge Q and the frequency of rotation

How to apply. Find the number of times per second the charge passes a fixed point of its path: that number is f. Then I = Qf.

Example. A ring with 10 μC spinning at 50 revolutions per second: I = 10 × 10⁻⁶ × 50 = 5 × 10⁻⁴ A = 0.5 mA. An electron making 6.6 × 10¹⁵ revolutions per second: I = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 6.6 × 10¹⁵ = 1.06 mA.

Drift velocity and mobility

  • vd = aτ = (eE)/(m) τ, where a = eE/m and τ = mean time between collisions (relaxation time)
  • mobility μ = (vd)/(E) = (eτ)/(m) (m²/V s)
  • I = nAeμE
  • vd ∝ E ∝ V/l, vd ∝ I/(nA)
  • vd ≈ 10⁻⁴ m/s while thermal speed ≈ 10⁵ m/s and the signal (field) travels at about c
  • when temperature rises τ falls, so the mobility and vd (at the same E) fall

How to apply. Use I = nAvd e to get vd from the current. For a fixed current, vd ∝ 1/A and vd ∝ 1/n. If E is given, vd = μE. Drift speed is tiny; the bulb lights at once because the field is set up at nearly the speed of light.

Example. Copper wire, I = 2 A, A = 1 mm², n = 8.5 × 10²⁸: vd = 2 ÷ (8.5 × 10²⁸ × 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 10⁻⁶) = 1.47 × 10⁻⁴ m/s. With E = ρJ = 1.7 × 10⁻⁸ × 2 × 10⁶ = 0.034 V/m, μ = 1.47 × 10⁻⁴ ÷ 0.034 = 4.3 × 10⁻³ m²/V s. In a 2 m wire the electron takes 2 ÷ 1.47 × 10⁻⁴ ≈ 1.4 × 10⁴ s (3.8 h) to cross it.

Conductivity and relaxation time

  • J = σE (microscopic Ohm’s law)
  • σ = (1)/(ρ) = (ne²τ)/(m) = neμ
  • ρ = (m)/(ne²τ)
  • in a semiconductor σ = e(neμe + nhμh)
  • for a metal ρ rises with temperature because τ falls (n is almost constant)
  • for a semiconductor ρ falls because n rises quickly

How to apply. Write ρ = m/(ne²τ). Then compare two situations by looking at which of n and τ has changed. In a metal only τ changes with temperature.

Example. Copper, n = 8.5 × 10²⁸, τ = 2.5 × 10⁻¹⁴ s: σ = 8.5 × 10²⁸ × (1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹)² × 2.5 × 10⁻¹⁴ ÷ 9.1 × 10⁻³¹ = 5.98 × 10⁷ S/m, so ρ = 1.67 × 10⁻⁸ Ω m. If τ is halved, ρ doubles.

Resistance and resistivity

Ohm’s law, resistance and resistivity

  • V = IR for an ohmic conductor (V–I graph is a straight line through the origin)
  • R = (ρl)/(A) = (l)/(σA)
  • ρ depends on the material and temperature only, R also on l and A
  • R = (ml)/(ne²τA)
  • V = IR is also written as E = ρJ
  • conductance G = 1/R (siemens)
  • 1 Ω = 1 V/A

How to apply. First find R from the shape (R = ρl/A), then the current or voltage from V = IR. For equal lengths and equal potential difference, I ∝ A/ρ.

Example. A copper wire (ρ = 1.7 × 10⁻⁸ Ω m) 10 m long with A = 1 mm²: R = 1.7 × 10⁻⁸ × 10 ÷ 10⁻⁶ = 0.17 Ω. At 3 A the potential drop is 0.51 V.

Stretching, cutting and joining a wire

  • The volume lV = Al is constant when a wire is stretched or drawn
  • stretched to n times the length: A becomes A/n, R′ = n²R
  • R ∝ l² ∝ 1/A² ∝ 1/r⁴ at constant volume
  • small change: (ΔR)/(R) ≈ 2(Δl)/(l)
  • a wire cut into n equal pieces: each has R/n, and joined in parallel gives R/n²
  • same material and mass: R ∝ l²

How to apply. At constant volume write R = ρl²/V. For a wire stretched by a given percent use ΔR/R = 2Δl/l (valid for small changes). For equal mass compare lengths squared.

Example. A 10 Ω wire is drawn to 3 times its length: R′ = 9 × 10 = 90 Ω. A 16 Ω wire cut into 4 equal parts: each is 4 Ω, and the four in parallel give 4 ÷ 4 = 1 Ω = 16 ÷ 4². If l increases by 1 % at constant volume, R increases by 2 %.

Temperature dependence of resistance

  • R = R₀(1 + αΔT), ρ = ρ₀(1 + αΔT)
  • (R₂)/(R₁) = (1 + αt₂)/(1 + αt₁) when R₀ is the value at 0 °C
  • conductors: α > 0 (R rises); semiconductors, insulators, carbon, electrolytes: α < 0 (R falls)
  • nichrome, manganin, constantan have very small α and large ρ (standard resistors, heaters)
  • series combination with no change of R: R₁α₁ + R₂α₂ = 0 (one with +α, one with −α)
  • a small-α alloy is used for resistance boxes

How to apply. Use the linear formula with the reference temperature at which α is given. For two temperatures use the ratio form. If the question gives R at two temperatures, find α first.

Example. R = 10 Ω at 20 °C and α = 0.004 K⁻¹: R(70 °C) = 10 (1 + 0.004 × 50) = 12 Ω. The resistance doubles when αΔT = 1, that is ΔT = 250 K.

Colour code of carbon resistors

  • bands in order: 1st digit, 2nd digit, multiplier (×10ⁿ), tolerance
  • colours = digits: black 0, brown 1, red 2, orange 3, yellow 4, green 5, blue 6, violet 7, grey 8, white 9
  • tolerance: gold ±5 %, silver ±10 %, no band ±20 %
  • R = (10a + b) × 10ⁿ Ω ± tolerance
  • a gold multiplier is ×0.1, silver ×0.01

How to apply. Read from the end that has the bands closer together. Write the two digits, then the power of ten, then the tolerance. The value lies between R(1 − tol) and R(1 + tol).

Example. Yellow, violet, red, gold: 47 × 10² = 4700 Ω ± 5 %, so between 4465 Ω and 4935 Ω. Brown, black, orange, silver: 10 × 10³ = 10 kΩ ± 10 %.

Resistance of non-uniform conductors

  • divide into thin slices and add: R = ∫(ρ dx)/(A(x))
  • truncated cone with end radii a and b, length l: R = (ρl)/(πab)
  • thin cylindrical shell between radii a and b, length l, current flowing radially: R = (ρ)/(2πl) ln(b)/(a)
  • concentric spherical shells: R = (ρ)/(4π)((1)/(a) − (1)/(b))

How to apply. Choose a thin slice of thickness dx (or dr) at a distance x (or r). Its resistance is ρ dx/A, with A as a function of position. Add (integrate) the slices because the current passes through them one after the other.

Example. Cone with ρ = 2 × 10⁻⁶ Ω m, l = 0.1 m, a = 1 mm, b = 2 mm: R = 2 × 10⁻⁶ × 0.1 ÷ (π × 10⁻³ × 2 × 10⁻³) = 0.032 Ω. Radial flow, ρ = 100 Ω m, a = 1 cm, b = 2 cm, l = 1 m: R = 100 × ln2 ÷ (2π) = 11.0 Ω.

Non-ohmic conductors: static and dynamic resistance

  • static resistance R = (V)/(I) (the chord from the origin)
  • dynamic (differential) resistance r = (dV)/(dI) (the slope of the tangent of the V–I graph)
  • ohmic: both are equal and constant
  • diode, filament, thermistor: non-linear V–I graph
  • lamp filament: R rises with I because the temperature rises
  • terminal graph of a cell: V = E − Ir, a line of slope −r

How to apply. Read the V and I at the point asked. For a formula I(V), use R = V/I for the static value and r = 1/(dI/dV) for the dynamic one.

Example. A device obeys I = 0.02 V² (A, V in volts). At V = 5 V: I = 0.5 A, static R = 5 ÷ 0.5 = 10 Ω; dI/dV = 0.04 V = 0.2 A/V, so dynamic r = 5 Ω.

Combinations of resistors

Resistors in series

  • Rₑq = R₁ + R₂ + R₃ + …
  • same current I in each
  • V divides in the ratio of resistances: V₁ = V(R₁)/(R₁ + R₂)
  • power in each P ∝ R
  • Rₑq is greater than the largest one
  • a switch or ideal ammeter in series adds no resistance

How to apply. Redraw the circuit, add the resistors that carry the same current, then find I = V/Rₑq and the drop across each.

Example. 2 Ω, 3 Ω and 5 Ω in series across 20 V: Rₑq = 10 Ω, I = 2 A, the drops are 4 V, 6 V and 10 V (they add to 20 V).

Resistors in parallel

  • (1)/(Rₑq) = (1)/(R₁) + (1)/(R₂) + …
  • two resistors: Rₑq = (R₁R₂)/(R₁ + R₂)
  • n equal resistors: R/n
  • same potential difference V across each
  • current divides inversely as resistance: I₁ = I(R₂)/(R₁ + R₂)
  • Rₑq is less than the smallest one
  • power in each P ∝ 1/R

How to apply. Find the points at the same potential. Add the conductances (1/R). For two branches use the current-divider formula, taking the other resistance in the numerator.

Example. 6 Ω and 3 Ω in parallel: Rₑq = 18 ÷ 9 = 2 Ω. With 9 A in the main line: I₆ = 9 × 3 ÷ 9 = 3 A and I₃ = 9 × 6 ÷ 9 = 6 A (they add to 9 A).

Balanced networks and equipotential points

  • if two points have the same potential, no current flows between them: remove the resistor joining them (or short them, the result is the same)
  • balanced Wheatstone bridge: (P)/(Q) = (R)/(S), the middle resistor carries no current
  • use the symmetry of the figure: points that are mirror images across the line joining the battery terminals have equal potential
  • a wire of zero resistance joining two points makes them one point (short circuit)

How to apply. Mark the potentials. By symmetry or by the ratio test find the points at equal potential and remove (or merge) accordingly. Then reduce by series and parallel steps.

Example. Bridge with arms 2 Ω, 4 Ω (top) and 3 Ω, 6 Ω (bottom) and a 10 Ω resistor between the middle points: 2/4 = 3/6, so it is balanced and the 10 Ω carries no current. Rₑq = (2 + 4) ∥ (3 + 6) = 6 ∥ 9 = 3.6 Ω.

Cube of resistors

  • twelve edges, each of resistance R
  • across an edge (adjacent corners): (7R)/(12)
  • across a face diagonal: (3R)/(4)
  • across a body diagonal (opposite corners): (5R)/(6)
  • for a body diagonal the three neighbours of each corner are at the same potential and may be joined

How to apply. Join the points of equal potential layer by layer: for the body diagonal there are 3 edges in parallel, then 6 in parallel, then 3 in parallel: R/3 + R/6 + R/3 = 5R/6.

Example. Each edge is 12 Ω: edge 7 Ω, face diagonal 9 Ω, body diagonal 10 Ω.

Infinite ladder

  • if the network repeats without end, removing one section does not change Rₑq
  • series arm a, shunt arm b: Rₑq = a + (b Rₑq)/(b + Rₑq) so Rₑq² − aRₑq − ab = 0
  • Rₑq = (a + √(a² + 4ab))/(2)
  • for a = b = R: Rₑq = (1 + √5)/(2) R ≈ 1.618R
  • it is larger than a

How to apply. Call the unknown total resistance x. The part to the right of the first section also has the value x. Write x = a + (b ∥ x), solve the quadratic and reject the negative root.

Example. a = 2 Ω and b = 4 Ω: x² − 2x − 8 = 0, so x = 4 Ω (check: 2 + 4 ∥ 4 = 2 + 2 = 4 Ω). For a = b = 2 Ω: x = 2 × 1.618 = 3.24 Ω.

Star–delta and uniform ring

  • delta to star: arm = (product of the two delta sides meeting there)/(sum of the three sides)
  • three equal delta sides R give star arms R/3
  • uniform ring of resistance R, points dividing it into arcs of x and (1 − x) of the ring: Rₑq = x(1 − x)R
  • diametrically opposite points: R/4
  • the same idea for a wire bent into a loop

How to apply. Replace the triangle by a star when no pair is in series or parallel. For a ring write the two arcs as two parallel branches.

Example. Three 9 Ω resistors in a triangle: each star arm is 3 Ω, so between two corners Rₑq = 6 Ω (check: 9 ∥ 18 = 6 Ω). A ring of 16 Ω: between points 90° apart the arcs are 4 Ω and 12 Ω, Rₑq = 4 × 12 ÷ 16 = 3 Ω; between opposite points 16/4 = 4 Ω.

Cells and EMF

EMF, internal resistance and terminal voltage

  • E = work done per unit charge by the cell (the open-circuit voltage)
  • I = (E)/(R + r)
  • discharging: V = E − Ir
  • charging: V = E + Ir and I = (V − E)/(R + r)
  • open circuit: V = E
  • short circuit: I = (E)/(r)
  • V–I graph: intercept E on the V axis, slope −r
  • V = (ER)/(R + r)

How to apply. Add the internal resistance in series with the external resistance to get I. The terminal voltage is the voltage across the external circuit. During charging the current enters the positive terminal and V > E.

Example. E = 12 V, r = 1 Ω and R = 5 Ω: I = 12 ÷ 6 = 2 A, V = 12 − 2 × 1 = 10 V. A 12 V battery (r = 1 Ω) charged from 15 V through 2 Ω: I = 3 ÷ 3 = 1 A, terminal voltage 12 + 1 = 13 V.

Cells in series

  • n identical cells: Eₑq = nE, rₑq = nr
  • I = (nE)/(R + nr)
  • good when R ≫ r
  • cells with opposite polarity subtract: Eₑq = ΣE (taken with signs), rₑq = Σr always adds
  • a cell with reversed terminals is being charged by the others

How to apply. Take the sum of EMFs with signs (going round the loop) and the sum of all internal resistances.

Example. 3 cells, each 1.5 V and 0.5 Ω, with R = 2.5 Ω: I = 4.5 ÷ (2.5 + 1.5) = 1.125 A. A 12 V (1 Ω) cell opposed to a 6 V (2 Ω) cell with R = 3 Ω: I = (12 − 6) ÷ (3 + 1 + 2) = 1 A.

Cells in parallel

  • m identical cells: Eₑq = E, rₑq = (r)/(m)
  • I = (E)/(R + r/m)
  • good when R ≪ r
  • unequal cells: Eₑq = (E₁/r₁ + E₂/r₂)/(1/r₁ + 1/r₂), rₑq = (r₁r₂)/(r₁ + r₂)
  • the EMF does not increase; only the current capacity and the lowering of r

How to apply. For identical cells the EMF is that of one cell. For unequal cells use the node-voltage form above. Check the current in each cell.

Example. Cells 4 V (1 Ω) and 2 V (1 Ω) in parallel: Eₑq = (4 + 2) ÷ 2 = 3 V, rₑq = 0.5 Ω. Four identical 1.5 V, 2 Ω cells in parallel across 0.5 Ω: I = 1.5 ÷ (0.5 + 0.5) = 1.5 A (one cell alone gives 0.6 A).

Mixed grouping of cells

  • N = mn cells: n in series in each row, m rows in parallel
  • I = (nmE)/(mR + nr)
  • the current is greatest when the total internal resistance equals the external one: R = (nr)/(m)
  • then Imax = (nE)/(2R) = (E√N)/(2√(rR))

How to apply. Put N = mn into I and choose n and m so that nr/m is as near as possible to R. If R ≫ r use all in series; if R ≪ r use all in parallel.

Example. 24 cells, E = 2 V, r = 1 Ω each, R = 6 Ω: need n/m = R/r = 6 with nm = 24, so n = 12 and m = 2. I = 24 × 2 ÷ (2 × 6 + 12 × 1) = 48 ÷ 24 = 2 A.

Power in a circuit and maximum power transfer

  • P(load) = I²R = (E²R)/((R + r)²)
  • maximum when R = r: Pₘₐₓ = (E²)/(4r)
  • efficiency η = (R)/(R + r) = (V)/(E) = 50 % at maximum power
  • total power of the cell = EI, lost in the cell I²r
  • P(load) is the same for two values R₁ and R₂ if R₁R₂ = r²
  • a graph of P against R rises, peaks at R = r and falls

How to apply. Write P(R). For the maximum set R = r. If a particular R is given, put it in the formula. Remember that maximum power is not maximum efficiency.

Example. E = 12 V, r = 3 Ω: at R = 3 Ω P = 144 ÷ 12 = 12 W (maximum). At R = 12 Ω, P = 144 × 12 ÷ 15² = 7.68 W. R = 1 Ω and R = 9 Ω give the same power (1 × 9 = 3²): 144 × 1 ÷ 16 = 9 W and 144 × 9 ÷ 144 = 9 W.

Kirchhoff and bridges

Kirchhoff’s laws

  • junction (current) law: ΣIᵢₙ = ΣIₒᵤₜ (charge is conserved)
  • loop (voltage) law: ΣΔV = 0 round any closed loop (energy is conserved)
  • signs: going through a resistor along the current ΔV = −IR, against it +IR
  • through a cell from − to +: +E, from + to −: −E
  • a negative answer means the current is opposite to the assumed direction

How to apply. Assume a current in each branch, apply the junction law at one node, then the loop law to as many independent loops as there are unknowns. Solve, and check with the junction law. For two nodes the node-voltage method is quicker.

Example. Branch 1: 8 V cell and 1 Ω; branch 2: 4 V cell and 2 Ω; load 2 Ω, all between the same two nodes. With the lower node at 0 V and the upper at V: (8 − V)/1 + (4 − V)/2 = V/2, so V = 5 V. I₁ = 3 A, I₂ = −0.5 A (the 4 V cell is being charged), load current 2.5 A (3 − 0.5 = 2.5 A).

Wheatstone bridge

  • balanced when no current flows through the galvanometer: (P)/(Q) = (R)/(S)
  • so for an unknown S: S = R(Q)/(P)
  • at balance the bridge is the same as two parallel branches (P + Q) and (R + S)
  • the bridge is most sensitive when all four arms are of the same order
  • when it is not balanced, use Kirchhoff’s laws (or the star–delta change)

How to apply. Check P/Q = R/S first. If balanced, remove the galvanometer arm. If not, assign currents and use the loop law, or use the Wheatstone network as two loops.

Example. P = 10 Ω, Q = 20 Ω, R = 15 Ω: S = R Q/P = 15 × 20 ÷ 10 = 30 Ω. Rₑq of the balanced bridge = 30 ∥ 45 = 18 Ω.

Meter bridge

  • uniform wire of length 100 cm: resistance proportional to length
  • balance at length l from the left end: (resistance in the left gap)/(resistance in the right gap) = (l)/(100 − l)
  • unknown X in the left gap: X = R(l)/(100 − l)
  • unknown X in the right gap: X = R(100 − l)/(l)
  • specific resistance ρ = (Xπd²)/(4L)
  • best accuracy when l is near 50 cm
  • interchanging X and R and averaging removes end errors
  • the heating of the wire must be avoided (press the key briefly)

How to apply. Be sure which gap holds the unknown. The segment next to a resistor is in proportion to that resistor: (left resistance) / (right resistance) = l/(100 − l). Use balance near 50 cm.

Example. R = 10 Ω in the right gap, X in the left gap, balance at l = 40 cm: X = 10 × 40 ÷ 60 = 6.67 Ω. With X in the right gap instead: X = 10 × 60 ÷ 40 = 15 Ω. Wire of length 0.5 m and diameter 1 mm with X = 15 Ω: ρ = 15 × π × (10⁻³)² ÷ (4 × 0.5) = 2.36 × 10⁻⁵ Ω m.

Potentiometer and meters

Potentiometer: principle and potential gradient

  • a long uniform wire of length L with a steady current: potential difference V ∝ length
  • potential gradient k = (V)/(L) = (I ρ)/(A) = (E R(wire))/((R(wire) + Rₕ + r) L)
  • a balanced cell of EMF E: E = kl (no current drawn from the cell at balance, so it measures the true EMF)
  • sensitivity rises when k is smaller (a series resistance Rₕ, longer wire)
  • the driver cell EMF must be greater than the EMF measured
  • the potentiometer is an ideal voltmeter

How to apply. Find the current in the wire from the driving circuit (E of the driver divided by the total resistance), then k = I × (resistance per unit length). The balancing length gives the unknown: E = kl.

Example. Wire of 10 m and 20 Ω, driver cell 3 V (no internal resistance) and rheostat 10 Ω in series: I = 3 ÷ 30 = 0.1 A, V(wire) = 2 V, k = 0.2 V/m. A cell balancing at 4.5 m has E = 0.2 × 4.5 = 0.9 V.

Potentiometer: comparing EMFs and internal resistance

  • comparing two cells: (E₁)/(E₂) = (l₁)/(l₂)
  • sum and difference method: (E₁ + E₂)/(E₁ − E₂) = (l₁)/(l₂) (aiding and opposing)
  • internal resistance: l₁ is the balance with the cell on open circuit (E = kl₁), l₂ with a known R across it (V = kl₂): r = R(l₁ − l₂)/(l₂)
  • the cell must give no current when l₁ is found
  • the reading with R gives the terminal voltage

How to apply. Open circuit gives E ∝ l₁; closing the key across R gives V = E R/(R + r) ∝ l₂. The ratio l₁/l₂ = (R + r)/R gives r.

Example. Balancing lengths 60 cm and 40 cm: E₁/E₂ = 3/2, so 1.5 V and 1.0 V. l₁ = 5 m, l₂ = 4 m with R = 8 Ω across the cell: r = 8 × (5 − 4) ÷ 4 = 2 Ω.

Galvanometer to ammeter: the shunt

  • a small resistance S (the shunt) is connected in parallel with the galvanometer (resistance G, full-scale current Ig)
  • S = (Ig G)/(I − Ig) = (G)/(n − 1) for a range I = nIg
  • ammeter resistance Rₐ = (GS)/(G + S) ≈ S (very small)
  • connected in series with the circuit
  • an ideal ammeter has zero resistance

How to apply. The galvanometer takes Ig and the shunt takes I − Ig at the same potential difference: Ig G = (I − Ig) S. Solve for S.

Example. G = 100 Ω, Ig = 1 mA, range 1 A: S = 0.001 × 100 ÷ 0.999 = 0.1001 Ω ≈ 0.1 Ω, and Rₐ ≈ 0.1 Ω. For a range 10 times larger than Ig: S = G/9.

Galvanometer to voltmeter: the series resistance

  • a large resistance Rₛ in series with the galvanometer
  • Rₛ = (V)/(Ig) − G
  • voltmeter resistance Rᵥ = G + Rₛ = (V)/(Ig) (very large)
  • connected in parallel across the element
  • an ideal voltmeter has infinite resistance
  • a range extended n times (V = nVg): Rₛ = (n − 1)G

How to apply. Full-scale: V = Ig (G + Rₛ). Solve for Rₛ. The voltmeter resistance is the full V divided by Ig.

Example. G = 50 Ω, Ig = 2 mA, range 10 V: Rₛ = 10 ÷ 0.002 − 50 = 4950 Ω and Rᵥ = 5000 Ω.

Errors due to the meters

  • voltmeter across R: reading is across R ∥ Rᵥ, so R(measured) = (R Rᵥ)/(R + Rᵥ) < R
  • ammeter in series: R(measured) = R + Rₐ > R
  • for a small R use the ammeter outside the voltmeter, for a large R put the voltmeter outside
  • the voltmeter reads less than the true value because it draws current; the ammeter reads less because it adds resistance

How to apply. Treat the meter as a resistor of its own resistance and recalculate the circuit.

Example. R = 1000 Ω with a 9000 Ω voltmeter across it: the circuit sees 1000 ∥ 9000 = 900 Ω, a 10 % error. A 0.5 Ω ammeter in series with a 10 Ω resistor reads 10.5 Ω.

Moving-coil galvanometer: sensitivity

  • deflection θ = (NBA)/(k) I (k = torsion constant, radial field)
  • current sensitivity (θ)/(I) = (NBA)/(k)
  • voltage sensitivity (θ)/(V) = (NBA)/(kR)
  • doubling N doubles the current sensitivity, but R doubles too, so the voltage sensitivity is unchanged
  • a radial field and soft iron core make θ ∝ I
  • increasing sensitivity: large N, B, A, small k

How to apply. Use θ ∝ I. For the effect of changing the coil, list how N, A, R change and apply the formula.

Example. N = 50, B = 0.05 T, A = 2 × 10⁻⁴ m², k = 10⁻⁶ N m/rad: θ/I = 50 × 0.05 × 2 × 10⁻⁴ ÷ 10⁻⁶ = 500 rad/A = 0.5 rad/mA. With R = 50 Ω: θ/V = 10 rad/V.

Power and heating

Electric power, energy and bulbs

  • P = VI = I²R = (V²)/(R)
  • energy W = Pt; 1 kWh = 3.6 × 10⁶ J
  • rated bulb (V, P): R = (V²)/(P)
  • at a different voltage V′: P′ = P(V′²)/(V²) (R assumed constant)
  • in series P ∝ R: the bulb of lower rating (higher R) glows brighter
  • in parallel P ∝ 1/R: the bulb of higher rating glows brighter
  • power is greatest when the resistance is smallest at a fixed V

How to apply. Find each R from its rating, then the current in the actual circuit and the power with the form that suits the circuit: I²R for series, V²/R for parallel.

Example. 100 W and 60 W bulbs (220 V): R₁ = 484 Ω, R₂ = 806.7 Ω. In series across 220 V: I = 220 ÷ 1290.7 = 0.1704 A, P₁ = I²R₁ = 14.1 W and P₂ = 23.4 W, so the 60 W bulb is brighter. A 100 W bulb on 110 V gives 25 W.

Joule heating

  • H = I²Rt = VIt = (V²t)/(R) (joule)
  • H (cal) = (I²Rt)/(4.2)
  • heating a body: Pt × η = ms ΔT
  • the heat in resistors in series is ∝ R, in parallel ∝ 1/R
  • the heat produced in a wire of fixed length and V falls if R rises
  • power loss in transmission lines = I²R, so the voltage is raised and I lowered

How to apply. Equate the heat to the energy needed, ms ΔT (add latent heat for a change of state). Allow for the efficiency η.

Example. A 1 kW heater warms 2 kg of water by 40 K: t = 2 × 4200 × 40 ÷ 1000 = 336 s. If only 80 % of the heat reaches the water, t = 420 s. 2 Ω and 3 Ω in parallel give heat in the ratio 3 : 2.

Energy bill and units of electricity

  • energy in kWh = (P(watt) × t(hour))/(1000)
  • cost = units × rate
  • P = VI, so a 2 kW heater on 220 V draws 9.1 A
  • the electricity meter measures energy (kWh), not power
  • 1 unit = 1 kWh = 3.6 × 10⁶ J

How to apply. Add up P × t for every appliance, divide by 1000 and multiply by the rate.

Example. 5 bulbs of 40 W for 6 h a day for 30 days: 200 W × 6 h = 1.2 kWh a day, 36 kWh in the month; at ₹6 per unit the bill is ₹216.

RC circuits and household wiring

Charging a capacitor through a resistor

  • time constant τ = RC
  • charging: q = CE(1 − e^−t/τ), Vᴄ = E(1 − e^−t/τ), I = (E)/(R) e^−t/τ
  • at t = 0 the capacitor acts as a short (I = E/R); at t → ∞ as an open circuit (I = 0)
  • after t = τ the charge is 63 % of the final value, after 2τ 86 %
  • energy supplied by the cell CE², half stored (½CE²) and half lost as heat in R, independent of R (C and U = ½CV²: see Electrostatics)

How to apply. Find the equivalent resistance seen by the capacitor, then τ = RC. Put t/τ in the exponential. Use the initial and final values (0 and ∞) to check the answer.

Example. R = 1 MΩ, C = 2 μF: τ = 2 s. At t = 2 s the capacitor holds 63.2 % of its final charge. With E = 10 V the cell supplies CE² = 2 × 10⁻⁶ × 100 = 200 μJ, of which 100 μJ is stored and 100 μJ becomes heat.

Discharging and the steady state

  • discharging: q = q₀e^−t/τ, I = I₀e^−t/τ
  • half of the charge is left after t = τ ln 2 = 0.693τ
  • in the steady state no current flows through a capacitor branch: remove it, find the currents, then Vᴄ = the voltage across the branch it is connected to
  • q = CVᴄ
  • then redraw for t = 0 (capacitor = short if uncharged)

How to apply. For the steady state, replace each capacitor by an open circuit. The voltage across the capacitor is that across the resistor (or part) in parallel with it.

Example. τ = 2 s: the charge falls to half in 2 × 0.693 = 1.39 s. A 12 V cell, R₁ = 2 Ω in series with R₂ = 4 Ω, and a 3 μF capacitor across R₂: steady current 2 A, Vᴄ = 8 V, Q = 3 × 8 = 24 μC.

Household circuits and the fuse

  • appliances are in parallel across the 220 V supply, each at full voltage and working independently
  • I = (P)/(V) for each, and the total current is ΣP/V
  • fuse (live wire) of low melting point; the rating is a little above the normal current
  • fuse wire: the current at which it melts, I ∝ r^3/2 (heating I²ρl/πr² equals the loss from the surface 2πrl)
  • earth wire to the metal body protects from shock; MCB cuts the circuit in an overload or a short circuit
  • the switch goes in the live wire

How to apply. Add the powers of everything that is on, divide by the voltage to get the current and choose the next standard rating. For two fuse wires of the same material and length use I ∝ r^3/2.

Example. Heater 1.5 kW, iron 500 W and four 60 W bulbs on 220 V: 2240 W, I = 10.2 A, so a 15 A fuse suits. A fuse wire of radius 0.2 mm that blows at 5 A: for radius 0.4 mm the current is 5 × 2^3/2 = 14.1 A.

Common myths and the truth

  • Myth: Electrons in a wire move at the speed of light. Truth: The drift speed is about 10⁻⁴ m/s. The signal (the electric field) travels at nearly c, and that is why the lamp lights at once.
  • Myth: A battery supplies a fixed current. Truth: A battery supplies a nearly fixed EMF; the current depends on the total resistance of the circuit, I = E/(R + r).
  • Myth: The current gets used up as it passes through the resistors. Truth: The same current enters and leaves a resistor. The energy is transferred, and the potential drops.
  • Myth: Resistance depends only on the material of the wire. Truth: R = ρl/A depends on the length and area too; it is the resistivity ρ that is a property of the material and temperature.
  • Myth: When a wire is stretched to double the length its resistance doubles. Truth: The area halves as well, so R becomes four times.
  • Myth: In a series circuit the bulb of higher wattage is always brighter. Truth: In series the same I flows and P = I²R, so the bulb with the higher resistance (lower wattage) is brighter. In parallel the higher-wattage bulb is brighter.
  • Myth: Maximum power in the load means maximum efficiency. Truth: At R = r the power is maximum but the efficiency is only 50 %. Efficiency R/(R + r) rises with R.
  • Myth: A voltmeter reads the true EMF of a cell. Truth: A voltmeter draws some current, so it reads E − Ir. Only the potentiometer, which draws no current at balance, measures the EMF.
  • Myth: Resistance of every conductor rises with temperature. Truth: Metals have a positive α, but semiconductors, carbon and electrolytes have a negative α, and their resistance falls when they are heated.
  • Myth: Cells in parallel give a higher EMF. Truth: The EMF stays the same; the parallel group lowers the internal resistance and shares the current between the cells.

Mistakes to avoid

  • In a parallel group the equivalent resistance is smaller than the smallest one; in series larger than the largest.
  • Stretching a wire changes both l and A; R becomes n²R, not nR.
  • In a mixed grouping the EMF of a row is nE, not mnE.
  • Maximum power in the load (R = r) is not the maximum efficiency (which is for a large R).
  • In the meter bridge check which gap holds the unknown before writing X = R l/(100 − l).
  • The voltage across a cell is E − Ir only while it delivers current; it is E + Ir when it is charged.
  • Drift velocity is tiny, but the current starts at once everywhere in the wire.
  • In Kirchhoff’s loop rule, count the sign of the cell and the direction of the current in each element.
