Electrostatics: JEE Main Physics formulas and notes

Electrostatics (with capacitors) is one of the most important Class 12 chapters in JEE Mains: it usually gives 2 to 4 questions every year, almost always from capacitors (series, parallel, dielectrics, energy), Gauss’s law with spheres and shells, potential and potential energy of charges, and the dipole. The formulas are short; the marks come from choosing the correct symmetry and from sign care. About 14 hours is enough.

Quick revision

  • F = kq₁q₂/r², k = 9 × 10⁹ N m²/C², E = kq/r², F = qE
  • Dipole: axial (2kp)/(r³), equatorial (kp)/(r³), τ = pE sinθ, U = −pE cosθ
  • Gauss: Φ = (q(in))/(ε₀); sphere E = (kQ)/(r²); line (λ)/(2πε₀r); sheet (σ)/(2ε₀)
  • Solid sphere inside E = (kQr)/(R³); shell inside E = 0; V = (kQ)/(R) inside
  • V = (kq)/(r), U = (kq₁q₂)/(r), E = −(dV)/(dr), W = qΔV, K = qV
  • Ring axis: E = (kQx)/((R² + x²)^3/2), V = (kQ)/(√(R² + x²))
  • C = (Q)/(V); plates (ε₀A)/(d); sphere 4πε₀R; spherical (4πε₀ab)/(b − a)
  • Series (1)/(C) = Σ(1)/(Cᵢ); parallel C = ΣCᵢ; U = ½CV² = (Q²)/(2C)
  • Slab: C = (ε₀A)/(d − t + t/K); u = ½ε₀E²; force between plates (Q²)/(2ε₀A)
  • Joining: V = (C₁V₁ + C₂V₂)/(C₁ + C₂), loss (C₁C₂(V₁ − V₂)²)/(2(C₁ + C₂)); RC: q = Q(1 − e^−t/RC)

Formulas with solved examples

Charge and Coulomb’s law

Properties of charge

  • charge is quantised: q = ne, n a whole number, e = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C (quarks of ±e/3 and ±2e/3 are never free)
  • charge is conserved in an isolated system and is additive (a scalar, added with sign)
  • charge does not depend on speed (mass does)
  • a body gets charged by gaining (negative) or losing (positive) electrons
  • m(e) = 9.1 × 10⁻³¹ kg, m(p) = 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg
  • 1 C = 6.25 × 10¹⁸ electrons

How to apply. Convert between charge and the number of electrons with n = q ÷ e. Add charges with their signs; when two identical conductors touch, the total charge is shared equally.

Example. A body with 5 × 10¹² extra electrons has q = −5 × 10¹² × 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ = −8 × 10⁻⁷ C. A charge of 3.2 μC is 3.2 × 10⁻⁶ ÷ 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 2 × 10¹³ electrons.

Coulomb’s law

  • F = (kq₁q₂)/(r²), k = (1)/(4πε₀) = 9 × 10⁹ N m²/C²
  • vector form: F⃗₁₂ = (kq₁q₂)/(r²) r̂₁₂ (like charges repel, unlike attract)
  • ε₀ = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² C²/N m²
  • in a medium of dielectric constant K: F = (F₀)/(K) = (q₁q₂)/(4πε₀Kr²)
  • valid for point charges at rest; obeys Newton’s third law; central and conservative
  • electric force is far stronger than gravity: for two electrons F(e) ÷ F(g) = 4.2 × 10⁴²

How to apply. Put the magnitudes of the charges in the formula for the size, and take the direction from the signs. In a medium divide by K; if the same pair is moved from air to a liquid and the force drops to 1/K, the liquid has constant K.

Example. 2 μC and 3 μC at 30 cm in air: F = 9 × 10⁹ × 2 × 10⁻⁶ × 3 × 10⁻⁶ ÷ 0.3² = 0.6 N. In water (K = 80) the force is 0.6 ÷ 80 = 7.5 × 10⁻³ N.

Superposition and equilibrium of charges on a line

  • F⃗ = F⃗₁ + F⃗₂ + … (vector sum; the force between two charges is not changed by a third one)
  • a third charge is in equilibrium on the line joining q₁ and q₂ at x from q₁: same signs: between them, x = (d√q₁)/(√q₁ + √q₂)
  • opposite signs: outside the line, on the side of the smaller charge, x = (d√q₁)/(√q₂ − √q₁) (|q₂| > |q₁|)
  • all three free: the middle charge is q₀ = −(q₁q₂)/((√q₁ + √q₂)²) for outer charges q₁, q₂ of the same sign

How to apply. Equate the two forces on the third charge (kq₁q/x² = kq₂q/(d − x)²) to get √q₁ ÷ x = √q₂ ÷ (d − x). The position does not depend on the third charge or its sign. For all three to be free, also balance a force on an outer charge.

Example. +4 μC and +9 μC, 30 cm apart: x = 30 × 2 ÷ (2 + 3) = 12 cm from the 4 μC. For all three to be in equilibrium the middle charge is −(4 × 9) ÷ 5² = −1.44 μC.

Charged balls on strings and in a liquid

  • two identical charged balls hang from the same point on strings of length L: tanθ = (F)/(mg) with F = (kq²)/(x²) and x = 2L sinθ
  • small angle: x³ = (2kq²L)/(mg) = (q²L)/(2πε₀mg)
  • same angle in air and in a liquid of density σ (balls of density ρ): F is divided by K and the weight is reduced by buoyancy, so K = (ρ)/(ρ − σ)

How to apply. Draw the three forces on a ball (weight, tension, Coulomb force) and use tanθ = F ÷ mg. For a small angle replace tanθ by x ÷ 2L. When the angle is unchanged on immersing, equate the tangents: F ÷ mg in air with (F ÷ K) ÷ (mg − upthrust) in the liquid, which gives K(mg − upthrust) = mg.

Example. q = 10⁻⁸ C on each ball of mass 0.18 g, L = 1 m: x³ = 2 × 9 × 10⁹ × 10⁻¹⁶ × 1 ÷ (1.8 × 10⁻⁴ × 10) = 10⁻³ m³, so x = 0.10 m. Balls of density 1600 kg/m³ keep the same angle in oil of density 800 kg/m³ when K = 1600 ÷ 800 = 2.

Charges at the corners of a square or polygon

  • equal charges q at the corners of a square of side a: force on one corner = (kq²)/(a²)(√2 + (1)/(2)) directed away from the centre
  • force on a charge at the centre of a regular polygon of equal charges is zero
  • a charge Q at the centre keeps the system in equilibrium when Q = −(q(1 + 2√2))/(4)
  • if one charge of a symmetric set is removed, the force on the centre charge equals that charge’s force, reversed

How to apply. Resolve the forces of the neighbours along and perpendicular to the diagonal, using the symmetry, and balance the net outward push against the pull of the centre charge, which is a distance a/√2 away.

Example. Four charges of +4 μC at the corners of a square: the central charge needed is Q = −4 × (1 + 2.828) ÷ 4 = −3.83 μC.

Electric field

Electric field of a point charge

  • E = (kq)/(r²) (N/C or V/m) directed away from +q and towards −q
  • F⃗ = qE⃗ (force on −q is opposite to E⃗)
  • superposition: E⃗ = ΣE⃗ᵢ, a vector sum
  • in a medium E = (kq)/(Kr²)
  • E = 0 at a point where two equal like charges are at equal distances on opposite sides (the midpoint)
  • two unequal like charges: the neutral point is between them, at x = d√q₁ ÷ (√q₁ + √q₂) from q₁

How to apply. Find the size of each field from kq/r², draw its direction, resolve into x and y parts and add. A point of zero field exists only where the fields point in opposite directions.

Example. The field 0.3 m from a 2 nC charge: E = 9 × 10⁹ × 2 × 10⁻⁹ ÷ 0.09 = 200 N/C. The force on a 5 μC charge placed there is 200 × 5 × 10⁻⁶ = 10⁻³ N.

Two equal charges: field on the bisector

  • two charges q at (±a, 0): E on the perpendicular bisector at a distance y = (2kqy)/((a² + y²)^3/2), directed along y
  • maximum when y = (a)/(√2): E(max) = (4kq)/(3√3 a²)
  • for +q and −q the field on the bisector is the equatorial field (2kqa)/((a² + y²)^3/2), parallel to the line joining them
  • at the midpoint of equal like charges E = 0

How to apply. Add the two fields as vectors; the components along the line cancel by symmetry (like charges) or add (unlike charges). To find the maximum set dE/dy = 0.

Example. q = 2 μC at (±0.1 m, 0): at y = 0.1 m E = 2 × 9 × 10⁹ × 2 × 10⁻⁶ × 0.1 ÷ (0.02)^3/2 = 1.27 × 10⁶ N/C; the maximum is 4 × 9 × 10⁹ × 2 × 10⁻⁶ ÷ (3√3 × 0.01) = 1.39 × 10⁶ N/C, at y = 0.0707 m.

Electric field lines

  • lines start on positive charges and end on negative charges (or go to infinity)
  • the tangent gives the direction of E⃗; the density of lines is proportional to E
  • two lines never cross (E has one direction at a point); they never form closed loops in electrostatics
  • they leave a conductor at right angles
  • the number of lines is proportional to the charge
  • a charge released from rest follows a line only if the line is straight

How to apply. Use the picture to compare fields: closer lines mean a stronger field. Lines run from high potential to low potential.

Example. For +4q and −q, if 4n lines leave +4q then n of them end on −q, and 3n go to infinity.

Ring and arc

  • ring of charge Q and radius R, on the axis at x: E = (kQx)/((R² + x²)^3/2) along the axis
  • E = 0 at the centre; far away E = (kQ)/(x²)
  • maximum at x = (R)/(√2): E(max) = (2kQ)/(3√3 R²) ≈ 0.385 (kQ)/(R²)
  • arc of angle 2α at its centre: E = (2kλ sinα)/(R) = (kQ sinα)/(αR²)
  • semicircle: E = (2kλ)/(R) = (2kQ)/(πR²)
  • ring: potential at centre (kQ)/(R)
  • hollow hemispherical shell (σ): at the centre of its flat face E = (σ)/(4ε₀) along the axis, away from the shell, and V = (kQ)/(R)

How to apply. Use symmetry: only the component along the axis survives, so E = ∫dE cosθ with cosθ = x/√(R² + x²). For an arc the perpendicular components cancel, and the length of the chord (2R sinα) replaces the circumference.

Example. Ring Q = 10 nC, R = 0.1 m, x = 0.1 m: E = 9 × 10⁹ × 10⁻⁸ × 0.1 ÷ (0.02)^3/2 = 3182 N/C. The maximum on the axis is 2 × 9 × 10⁹ × 10⁻⁸ ÷ (3√3 × 0.01) = 3464 N/C.

Disc, sheet and plates

  • disc of radius R and surface density σ on the axis: E = (σ)/(2ε₀)(1 − (x)/(√(x² + R²)))
  • infinite sheet: E = (σ)/(2ε₀), the same at all distances
  • two sheets of +σ and −σ: E = (σ)/(ε₀) between, 0 outside
  • sheets σ₁ and σ₂ (same sign): between E = (σ₁ − σ₂)/(2ε₀), outside (σ₁ + σ₂)/(2ε₀)
  • just outside a conductor E = (σ)/(ε₀)

How to apply. Add the sheet fields as vectors with directions: away from a positive sheet, towards a negative one. The field of an infinite sheet does not depend on the distance; a disc behaves like a sheet when x ≪ R.

Example. σ = 2 μC/m²: sheet E = 2 × 10⁻⁶ ÷ (2 × 8.85 × 10⁻¹²) = 1.13 × 10⁵ N/C. A disc with R = 0.3 m at x = 0.4 m: E = 1.13 × 10⁵ × (1 − 0.4/0.5) = 2.26 × 10⁴ N/C.

Line charge and rod

  • infinite line of charge λ: E = (λ)/(2πε₀r) = (2kλ)/(r), directed away from the line
  • finite rod, point at distance r from the line of the rod: E⊥ = (kλ)/(r)(sinθ₁ + sinθ₂), where θ₁ and θ₂ are the angles from the perpendicular to the ends
  • on the axis of a rod of length L, at distance d from the near end: E = (kQ)/(d(d + L))
  • infinite rod: θ₁ = θ₂ = 90°

How to apply. Take a small element dq = λ dx, write its field and integrate with the angle as the variable. For the infinite line Gauss’s law with a cylinder gives the result faster.

Example. λ = 3 nC/m at r = 0.6 m: E = 2 × 9 × 10⁹ × 3 × 10⁻⁹ ÷ 0.6 = 90 N/C. A rod of 4 nC and length 0.2 m, point on its axis 0.1 m from the near end: E = 9 × 10⁹ × 4 × 10⁻⁹ ÷ (0.1 × 0.3) = 1200 N/C.

Charged particle in a uniform field

  • a = (qE)/(m) in the direction of E⃗ for +q
  • from rest through distance d: v = √(2qEd)/(m) = √(2qV)/(m)
  • entering at right angles to E with speed v₀: y = (qEx²)/(2mv₀²), parabola
  • deflection angle tanθ = (qEL)/(mv₀²) for plates of length L
  • work done W = qEd = ΔK
  • Millikan’s oil drop in balance: qE = mg, so q = (mg)/(E) (always a whole number of e)
  • time in the plates t = L/v₀

How to apply. Treat it like projectile motion: constant acceleration along E, constant speed across it. Neglect gravity for electrons and ions unless the problem includes the weight.

Example. An electron with v₀ = 2 × 10⁷ m/s enters plates 5 cm long with E = 2000 N/C: y = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 2000 × 0.05² ÷ (2 × 9.1 × 10⁻³¹ × (2 × 10⁷)²) = 1.1 × 10⁻³ m. An oil drop of 9.6 × 10⁻¹⁵ kg in E = 6 × 10⁵ N/C balances with q = 9.6 × 10⁻¹⁵ × 10 ÷ (6 × 10⁵) = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, one electron.

Small oscillations of a charge near a ring or a pair of charges

  • charge −q (mass m) displaced a small distance x along the axis of a ring of charge Q (radius R): E ≈ (kQx)/(R³), so F = −(kQq)/(R³)x and T = 2π√(4πε₀mR³)/(Qq)
  • charge −q on the bisector of two fixed charges Q at (±a, 0), displaced a small y along the bisector: F ≈ −(2kQqy)/(a³), T = 2π√(ma³)/(2kQq)
  • the moving charge must be opposite in sign to the fixed ones, otherwise it is pushed away

How to apply. Expand the field for a small displacement (x ≪ R, so (R² + x²)^3/2 ≈ R³), write F = −(constant)x and read ω² = constant ÷ m.

Example. Ring of 10 nC, R = 0.1 m, a particle of charge −1 nC and mass 1 mg: ω² = 9 × 10⁹ × 10⁻⁸ × 10⁻⁹ ÷ (10⁻³ × 10⁻⁶) = 90 s⁻², T = 2π ÷ √90 = 0.66 s.

Electric dipole

Field of a dipole

  • dipole moment p = q(2a), from −q to +q, in C m
  • axial point (distance r from the centre): E = (2kp)/(r³) for r ≫ a, exact (2kpr)/((r² − a²)²), along p
  • equatorial point: E = (kp)/(r³) for r ≫ a, exact (kp)/((r² + a²)^3/2), opposite to p
  • at angle θ from the axis: E = (kp)/(r³)√(1 + 3cos²θ), tanα = (tanθ)/(2) (α from the radial line)
  • E ∝ 1/r³ (faster than a point charge)
  • E(axial) = 2 E(equatorial) at the same distance, for r ≫ a only

How to apply. Find the distance r from the centre of the dipole and whether the point is on the axis or on the equatorial line. For any other angle resolve p into a component along the line and one perpendicular to it.

Example. q = 2 μC, 2a = 2 mm so p = 4 × 10⁻⁹ C m. At r = 0.2 m: axial E = 2 × 9 × 10⁹ × 4 × 10⁻⁹ ÷ 0.008 = 9000 N/C and equatorial E = 4500 N/C.

Dipole in an electric field: torque and energy

  • torque τ⃗ = p⃗ × E⃗, τ = pE sinθ, maximum pE at θ = 90°
  • potential energy U = −p⃗·E⃗ = −pE cosθ (minimum −pE at θ = 0, maximum +pE at θ = 180°, stable and unstable equilibrium)
  • work to turn from θ₁ to θ₂: W = pE(cosθ₁ − cosθ₂)
  • net force zero in a uniform field; in a non-uniform field F = p (dE)/(dx)
  • small oscillation about θ = 0: T = 2π√(I)/(pE)
  • potential of a dipole V = (kp cosθ)/(r²), zero on the equatorial plane

How to apply. Take θ as the angle between p⃗ and E⃗ and write U = −pE cosθ. The dipole turns to line up with E⃗ and oscillates in SHM if disturbed a little.

Example. p = 4 × 10⁻⁹ C m in E = 5000 N/C at 30°: τ = 4 × 10⁻⁹ × 5000 × 0.5 = 10⁻⁵ N m. Turning it from 0° to 90° costs W = pE = 2 × 10⁻⁵ J.

Gauss’s law

Electric flux and Gauss’s law

  • flux Φ = ∮E⃗·dA⃗ (N m²/C); uniform field through a plane: Φ = EA cosθ
  • Gauss’s law: Φ = (q(in))/(ε₀) for a closed surface
  • only the charge inside enters Φ, but E on the surface is due to all charges
  • the flux through a closed surface is zero if no charge is inside (or if E is uniform)
  • flux from a charge q through a surface subtending solid angle Ω: Φ = (q)/(ε₀)(Ω)/(4π)
  • a hemisphere of radius R in a uniform field along its axis: Φ = πR²E

How to apply. Choose a closed (Gaussian) surface through the point where E is wanted, with the same symmetry as the charge, so that E is constant on it and perpendicular or parallel to it. Then E × area = q(in) ÷ ε₀.

Example. A uniform field of 500 N/C at 60° to the normal through a plate of 0.2 m²: Φ = 500 × 0.2 × cos60° = 50 N m²/C. A closed surface enclosing +3 nC and −1 nC: Φ = 2 × 10⁻⁹ ÷ (8.85 × 10⁻¹²) = 226 N m²/C.

Flux through a cube, a disc and a surface

  • charge q at the centre of a cube: (q)/(6ε₀) through each face
  • q at a corner: (q)/(8ε₀) through the cube; the three faces meeting at the corner get 0, each of the other three (q)/(24ε₀)
  • q at the centre of a face: (q)/(2ε₀) through the cube
  • q on an edge: (q)/(4ε₀)
  • charge q on the axis of a disc of radius R at distance h: Φ = (q)/(2ε₀)(1 − (h)/(√(h² + R²)))
  • charge q at the centre of a sphere: all of (q)/(ε₀) through it, whatever the shape

How to apply. Use symmetry: imagine the charge placed at the centre of a larger cube made of identical cubes (8 for a corner, 4 for an edge, 2 for a face), then divide q/ε₀ by that number.

Example. q = 5.31 nC at the centre of a cube: each face has 5.31 × 10⁻⁹ ÷ (6 × 8.85 × 10⁻¹²) = 100 N m²/C. With q at a corner, each far face has 5.31 × 10⁻⁹ ÷ (24 × 8.85 × 10⁻¹²) = 25 N m²/C.

Spheres and shells

  • conducting sphere or thin shell (charge Q, radius R): E = 0 for r < R, E = (kQ)/(r²) for r ≥ R, at the surface (kQ)/(R²) = (σ)/(ε₀)
  • uniformly charged insulating (solid) sphere: E = (kQr)/(R³) = (ρr)/(3ε₀) inside, (kQ)/(r²) outside; E is largest at r = R
  • at r = R/2 inside E is half its surface value; at r = 2R it is a quarter
  • several shells: E at r = k × (charge inside r) ÷ r²

How to apply. Draw a spherical Gaussian surface of radius r and find the charge inside it. For a solid sphere that charge is Q(r/R)³; for a shell it is zero if r < R.

Example. Q = 8 μC, R = 0.2 m (solid insulating): at r = 0.1 m E = 9 × 10⁹ × 8 × 10⁻⁶ × 0.1 ÷ 0.2³ = 9 × 10⁵ N/C; at r = R it is 1.8 × 10⁶ N/C; at r = 0.4 m it is 4.5 × 10⁵ N/C.

Cylinder and slab

  • long charged line or cylinder surface (λ per length): E = (λ)/(2πε₀r) outside
  • uniformly charged solid cylinder (ρ, radius R): E = (ρr)/(2ε₀) inside, (ρR²)/(2ε₀r) outside
  • infinite plane sheet: (σ)/(2ε₀)
  • thick slab of density ρ and thickness d: inside at distance x from the middle plane E = (ρx)/(ε₀), outside E = (ρd)/(2ε₀)
  • hollow cylinder: E = 0 inside

How to apply. Use a coaxial cylinder (for line and cylinder) or a pillbox (for sheets and slabs) as the Gaussian surface. For the pillbox, flux comes out through both ends.

Example. A slab of ρ = 4 × 10⁻⁸ C/m³ and d = 0.2 m: outside E = 4 × 10⁻⁸ × 0.2 ÷ (2 × 8.85 × 10⁻¹²) = 452 N/C; at x = 0.05 m from the middle plane E = 4 × 10⁻⁸ × 0.05 ÷ (8.85 × 10⁻¹²) = 226 N/C.

Non-uniform charge density

  • charge inside radius r: q(r) = ∫ 4πr²ρ dr, then E = (kq(r))/(r²)
  • ρ = Ar (r ≤ R): q(r) = πAr⁴, E = (Ar²)/(4ε₀) inside (a parabola), total charge Q = πAR⁴
  • ρ = ρ₀(1 − r/R): E = (ρ₀)/(ε₀)((r)/(3) − (r²)/(4R)) inside, maximum (ρ₀R)/(9ε₀) at r = 2R/3, total Q = (πρ₀R³)/(3)
  • outside: E = kQ/r² as for a point charge
  • a uniform field inside a uniformly charged region would need ρ ∝ 1/r

How to apply. Integrate shell by shell: a thin shell of radius r and thickness dr carries 4πr²ρ dr. Then apply Gauss’s law with a sphere of radius r. Check that E is continuous at r = R.

Example. ρ = Ar with A = 2 × 10⁻⁶ C/m⁴ at r = 0.1 m: E = 2 × 10⁻⁶ × 0.01 ÷ (4 × 8.85 × 10⁻¹²) = 565 N/C.

Cavity in a charged sphere

  • sphere of uniform density ρ with a spherical cavity: the field inside the cavity is uniform, E⃗ = (ρ d⃗)/(3ε₀), where d⃗ points from the centre of the sphere to the centre of the cavity
  • for a long cylinder with a cylindrical cavity E⃗ = (ρ d⃗)/(2ε₀)
  • method: the cavity = solid sphere of +ρ plus a sphere of −ρ
  • outside the sphere the field is that of the sphere without cavity minus that of the missing charge

How to apply. Replace the cavity by two superposed spheres of density +ρ and −ρ. Inside the cavity E⃗ = ρr⃗₁/3ε₀ − ρr⃗₂/3ε₀ = ρ(r⃗₁ − r⃗₂)/3ε₀ = ρd⃗/3ε₀, which does not depend on the position in the cavity.

Example. ρ = 5 × 10⁻⁶ C/m³ and d = 4 cm: E = 5 × 10⁻⁶ × 0.04 ÷ (3 × 8.85 × 10⁻¹²) = 7530 N/C, the same at every point of the cavity.

Potential and potential energy

Potential, field and the relation between them

  • V = (W)/(q₀) = work per unit charge to bring it from infinity
  • point charge V = (kq)/(r) (with sign)
  • ΔV = V(B) − V(A) = −∫E⃗·dl⃗
  • E = −(dV)/(dr), E(x) = −(∂V)/(∂x) etc.; E⃗ = −∇V
  • in a uniform field V(A) − V(B) = Ed along E; E points towards lower V
  • V = J/C = volt; E in V/m = N/C
  • 1 eV = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ J

How to apply. To find E from V(x, y, z) differentiate with respect to each coordinate and put in the signs; to find V from E integrate along any path (the work is path independent).

Example. V = 2x² + 3y volt: E(x) = −4x, E(y) = −3; at x = 1 m, E = (−4, −3) V/m, and |E| = √(16 + 9) = 5 V/m.

Potential of charge distributions

  • V = k Σ (qᵢ)/(rᵢ), an algebraic sum (a scalar)
  • ring on the axis: V = (kQ)/(√(R² + x²)), at the centre (kQ)/(R) (also for any arc or polygon of equal distance)
  • disc on the axis V = (σ)/(2ε₀)(√(x² + R²) − x)
  • dipole V = (kp cosθ)/(r²), zero on the equatorial plane
  • long line: ΔV = V(r₁) − V(r₂) = (λ)/(2πε₀) ln(r₂)/(r₁)
  • infinite sheet: V decreases by (σ)/(2ε₀) per metre

How to apply. Add the potentials of all the charges with their signs, which is simpler than adding fields. For a continuous charge use dV = k dq/r.

Example. A ring of 10 nC and R = 0.1 m at x = 0.1 m: V = 9 × 10⁹ × 10⁻⁸ ÷ √0.02 = 636 V. The axial point of a dipole of p = 4 × 10⁻⁹ C m, r = 0.2 m: V = 900 V. A line of 3 nC/m: V(0.3) − V(0.6) = 2 × 9 × 10⁹ × 3 × 10⁻⁹ × ln 2 = 37.4 V.

Potential of a sphere and shells

  • conducting sphere or shell: V = (kQ)/(r) for r ≥ R, constant (kQ)/(R) for r ≤ R
  • uniformly charged insulating sphere: V = (kQ(3R² − r²))/(2R³) inside; at the centre (3kQ)/(2R) (1.5 times the surface value)
  • system of concentric shells: V(r) = k Σ (Qᵢ)/(max(r, Rᵢ))
  • inside a shell the potential is constant but the field is zero

How to apply. For a point inside a set of shells, charges on shells of radius smaller than r count as kQ/r and those on larger shells count as kQ/R. Sum all terms.

Example. Q = 8 μC, R = 0.2 m: shell V = 9 × 10⁹ × 8 × 10⁻⁶ ÷ 0.2 = 3.6 × 10⁵ V. Insulating sphere: centre 5.4 × 10⁵ V; r = 0.1 m gives 4.95 × 10⁵ V. Shells of +4 nC (0.1 m) and −2 nC (0.2 m): at r = 0.15 m V = 9 × 10⁹ × (4 × 10⁻⁹ ÷ 0.15 − 2 × 10⁻⁹ ÷ 0.2) = 150 V.

Potential energy of a system of charges

  • U = (kq₁q₂)/(r) for two charges (zero at infinity; negative for unlike charges)
  • U = k Σ (qᵢqⱼ)/(rᵢⱼ) over all pairs, each pair once
  • work done by an external agent to assemble the system = U
  • W(A→B) = q(V(B) − V(A)), independent of the path
  • three equal charges on a triangle of side a: (3kq²)/(a)
  • four equal charges on a square: (4 + √2)(kq²)/(a)

How to apply. List every pair, find its distance and add the pair energies with their signs. For N identical charges the number of pairs is N(N − 1)/2.

Example. Four charges of 2 μC at the corners of a square of side 0.3 m: U = (4 + 1.414) × 9 × 10⁹ × (2 × 10⁻⁶)² ÷ 0.3 = 0.650 J.

Work-energy theorem for charges

  • qΔV = ΔK
  • accelerated through V: v = √(2qV)/(m); an electron through 100 V gets 5.93 × 10⁶ m/s, and 100 eV
  • closest approach of a charge q to a fixed charge Q (head-on): K = (kQq)/(r(min))
  • two free particles released from r: ½m₁v₁² + ½m₂v₂² = (kq₁q₂)/(r) with m₁v₁ = m₂v₂
  • escape to infinity needs K ≥ (kq₁q₂)/(r) (like charges)
  • the speed is largest where V (times q) is least

How to apply. Conserve energy: K + U is constant, with U = qV. For two free bodies also conserve momentum. At the turning point the speed is zero (closest approach).

Example. A 5 MeV alpha particle (2e) heading for gold (79e): r(min) = 9 × 10⁹ × 79 × 2 × (1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹)² ÷ (5 × 1.6 × 10⁻¹³) = 4.55 × 10⁻¹⁴ m. Two 1 g particles with 2 μC each released at 0.2 m: each moves at v = √(9 × 10⁹ × 4 × 10⁻¹² ÷ (10⁻³ × 0.2)) = 13.4 m/s.

Equipotential surfaces and graphs of E and V

  • an equipotential surface has the same V everywhere; no work to move a charge on it
  • E⃗ is perpendicular to it and points to lower V; the surfaces never cross
  • a point charge gives concentric spheres; a uniform field gives parallel planes, spaced d = ΔV/E; the equatorial plane of a dipole is at 0 V
  • closer surfaces mean a stronger field
  • slope of V–x graph = −E; area under E–x graph = −ΔV
  • V(A) − V(B) = ∫E dx

How to apply. Read E from the slope of a V–x graph (with the minus sign), and V from the area under an E–x graph. The zero of E on a graph is where V has an extreme value.

Example. Planes of 10 V and 20 V are 5 cm apart in a uniform field: E = 10 ÷ 0.05 = 200 V/m. If E = 200 V/m up to 0.3 m and 100 V/m from 0.3 m to 0.5 m, V falls by 60 + 20 = 80 V over 0.5 m.

Self energy of a sphere or shell

  • conducting sphere or thin shell (Q, R): U = (kQ²)/(2R) = (Q²)/(8πε₀R)
  • uniformly charged solid insulating sphere: U = (3kQ²)/(5R)
  • energy stored in the field outside a sphere, from R to ∞: (kQ²)/(2R); inside the solid sphere: (kQ²)/(10R)
  • expanding a charged shell from R₁ to R₂ lowers U by (kQ²)/(2)((1)/(R₁) − (1)/(R₂)), the work done by the electric force

How to apply. Use u = ½ε₀E² integrated over the volume (shell element 4πr² dr) or build up the charge shell by shell with dW = V dq.

Example. A shell of 8 μC and R = 0.2 m: U = 9 × 10⁹ × (8 × 10⁻⁶)² ÷ (2 × 0.2) = 1.44 J. The same charge uniformly in a solid sphere: U = 3 × 9 × 10⁹ × (8 × 10⁻⁶)² ÷ (5 × 0.2) = 1.73 J.

Conductors

Conductors in electrostatic equilibrium

  • E = 0 inside; V is the same at every point, so the surface is an equipotential
  • excess charge lies only on the outer surface
  • E⃗ at the surface is perpendicular to it, E = (σ)/(ε₀)
  • charge density is larger where the surface is sharper (σ ∝ 1/R for spheres at the same V): corona discharge, lightning conductor
  • electrostatic shielding: the field in an empty cavity of a conductor is zero
  • outward pressure on the surface P = (σ²)/(2ε₀) = ½ε₀E²

How to apply. Use these facts as the starting rules: the potential of a conductor is one number, and its charge is on the outside. For the pressure on a charged soap bubble or a conducting sphere use P = ½ε₀E².

Example. A sphere of radius 0.1 m and charge 8 nC: E at the surface = 9 × 10⁹ × 8 × 10⁻⁹ ÷ 0.01 = 7200 N/C, and the outward pressure ½ε₀E² = 0.5 × 8.85 × 10⁻¹² × 7200² = 2.3 × 10⁻⁴ Pa.

Induced charges and shells with charges inside

  • charge q placed inside a cavity of a conducting shell: induced −q on the inner surface and +q more on the outer surface, whatever the position of q
  • shell of charge Q: outer surface carries Q + q
  • for a charged sphere (a, charge q) inside a shell of inner radius b and outer radius c with charge Q: V(sphere) = k((q)/(a) − (q)/(b) + (q + Q)/(c)), V(shell) = (k(q + Q))/(c)
  • V(sphere) − V(shell) = kq((1)/(a) − (1)/(b)) does not depend on Q
  • earthing the shell removes its outer charge: V(shell) = 0

How to apply. Add the induced charges layer by layer using Gauss’s law inside the metal (E = 0 there). Then find the potential by adding contributions from each surface. Earthing means V = 0, not Q = 0.

Example. a = 0.1 m, b = 0.2 m, c = 0.3 m, q = 5 nC on the inner sphere and Q = 3 nC on the shell: outer surface 8 nC, V(shell) = 9 × 10⁹ × 8 × 10⁻⁹ ÷ 0.3 = 240 V, V(sphere) = 240 + 9 × 10⁹ × 5 × 10⁻⁹ × (10 − 5) = 465 V. If the shell is earthed: V(sphere) = 225 V.

Spheres joined by a wire; charge sharing

  • two conducting spheres far apart, joined by a wire: same potential, V = (k(Q₁ + Q₂))/(R₁ + R₂)
  • q₁ : q₂ = R₁ : R₂
  • σ₁ : σ₂ = R₂ : R₁ (the smaller sphere has the larger surface density and field)
  • energy lost as heat: U(i) − U(f) with U = kq²/2R for each sphere
  • identical spheres share charge equally on touching
  • joining two capacitors of capacitance 4πε₀R is the same problem

How to apply. Total charge is conserved. Equal potential means kq₁/R₁ = kq₂/R₂. Compute the energies before and after to get the heat lost.

Example. A 0.1 m sphere with 6 μC joined to an uncharged 0.2 m sphere: V = 9 × 10⁹ × 6 × 10⁻⁶ ÷ 0.3 = 1.8 × 10⁵ V; charges 2 μC and 4 μC. Energy changes from 1.62 J to 0.18 + 0.36 = 0.54 J, so 1.08 J is lost.

Earthing and induced charge on an earthed sphere

  • an earthed conductor is at V = 0
  • a charged sphere (a) inside an earthed shell (b) induces −q on the shell’s inner surface, none outside: V(sphere) = kq((1)/(a) − (1)/(b))
  • joining the inner sphere to the shell with a wire puts all charge on the outer surface
  • earthed sphere of radius R with a point charge q at a distance d from its centre: induced charge Q′ = −(qR)/(d) (put V at the centre = 0: kQ′/R + kq/d = 0)
  • the potential at the centre of a conductor is the sum of the contributions of all charges, including the far ones

How to apply. Write the potential at a convenient point (usually the centre) as a sum of all contributions and set it equal to the given V (0 for earth). The charge on a surface far from the others is found from this single equation.

Example. An earthed sphere of R = 0.1 m and a point charge of +2 μC at 0.5 m from its centre: induced charge = −2 × 0.1 ÷ 0.5 = −0.4 μC.

Capacitance and combinations

Capacitance and units

  • C = (Q)/(V), Q the charge on the positive plate and V the potential difference between the plates
  • unit farad (1 F = 1 C/V); 1 μF = 10⁻⁶ F, 1 pF = 10⁻¹² F
  • C depends only on the size, shape, spacing and the medium; not on Q or V
  • isolated sphere of radius R: C = 4πε₀R = (R)/(k)
  • the Earth: C = 4πε₀ × 6.4 × 10⁶ m = 711 μF
  • dimension M⁻¹L⁻²T⁴A²

How to apply. Find the potential difference that a charge Q produces between the conductors (use Gauss’s law); C = Q/V is then constant. For a single conductor the second plate is at infinity.

Example. A sphere of radius 9 cm in air: C = 0.09 ÷ (9 × 10⁹) = 10⁻¹¹ F = 10 pF. At 1000 V it holds Q = 10 nC.

Parallel plate capacitor

  • C = (ε₀A)/(d) (with a dielectric K filling the gap: C = (Kε₀A)/(d))
  • E = (σ)/(ε₀) = (Q)/(ε₀A) = (V)/(d) between the plates
  • V = (Qd)/(ε₀A)
  • valid for d ≪ √A (edge effect neglected)
  • C ∝ A, C ∝ 1/d
  • charge Q on the positive plate; the inner faces carry ±Q, the outer faces of an isolated pair carry no charge

How to apply. Take the area of overlap and the separation in metres. A change of d, A or K changes C in the same proportion. Plate charges follow Q = CV.

Example. A = 100 cm² = 10⁻² m² and d = 1 mm: C = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² × 10⁻² ÷ 10⁻³ = 88.5 pF. At 100 V: Q = 8.85 nC and E = 100 ÷ 10⁻³ = 10⁵ V/m.

Spherical and cylindrical capacitors

  • spherical: inner radius a, outer radius b, outer shell earthed: C = (4πε₀ab)/(b − a) = (ab)/(k(b − a))
  • inner sphere earthed and outer shell charged: C = (4πε₀b²)/(b − a) (the sphere capacitor in parallel with the isolated shell 4πε₀b)
  • b → ∞: C = 4πε₀a
  • cylindrical (length L ≫ b): C = (2πε₀L)/(ln(b/a)), per metre (2πε₀)/(ln(b/a))
  • for a ≈ b the spherical capacitor is a parallel plate capacitor of area 4πa² and gap b − a

How to apply. Find V between the surfaces from V = kq(1/a − 1/b) for spheres, or from the line-charge field for cylinders, and use C = Q/V. Check which conductor is earthed.

Example. a = 0.1 m, b = 0.2 m: C = 0.1 × 0.2 ÷ (9 × 10⁹ × 0.1) = 22.2 pF. If the inner sphere is earthed instead: C = 4π × 8.85 × 10⁻¹² × 0.04 ÷ 0.1 = 44.5 pF. Cylinder a = 1 mm, b = 10 mm: C per metre = 5.56 × 10⁻¹¹ ÷ ln 10 = 24.1 pF/m.

Series and parallel combinations

  • parallel: C = C₁ + C₂ + …, same V, charge divides Q₁ : Q₂ = C₁ : C₂
  • series: (1)/(C) = (1)/(C₁) + (1)/(C₂) + …, same Q, voltage divides V₁ : V₂ = C₂ : C₁ (V₁ = V (C₂)/(C₁ + C₂))
  • two in series: (C₁C₂)/(C₁ + C₂)
  • n identical: parallel nC, series C/n
  • energy in series ∝ 1/C, in parallel ∝ C
  • balanced bridge ((C₁)/(C₂) = (C₃)/(C₄)): the middle capacitor has no charge

How to apply. Join the points of equal potential (a wire joins them), then simplify step by step. In series every capacitor has the same charge; in parallel every one has the same voltage. Find the total, then work backwards.

Example. 2 μF and 3 μF in series across 100 V: C = 6 ÷ 5 = 1.2 μF, Q = 120 μC on each, and V = 60 V on the 2 μF and 40 V on the 3 μF.

Parallel plates: charge on each face

  • a stack of isolated parallel conducting plates with charges Q₁, Q₂, … (equal areas, close together): each of the two outer faces carries (ΣQ)/(2)
  • each inner face then follows plate by plate: face on the right = plate charge − face on the left
  • facing surfaces of neighbouring plates are equal and opposite
  • field in a gap = (charge on the facing surface) ÷ (ε₀A)

How to apply. Find the total charge, put half on each outer face, then work plate by plate from one end, because E must be zero inside every metal plate.

Example. Plates of +6 μC and +2 μC: outer faces 4 μC each; plate 1 has 4 μC on its left face and 2 μC on its right face; plate 2 has −2 μC on its left face and 4 μC on its right face (sum 2 μC). The gap field is 2 × 10⁻⁶ ÷ (ε₀A).

Energy and dielectrics

Energy stored and energy density

  • U = ½CV² = (Q²)/(2C) = ½QV
  • energy density u = (U)/(volume) = ½ε₀E² in a vacuum, ½Kε₀E² = (ε E²)/(2) in a dielectric
  • U = u × Ad for a parallel plate capacitor
  • a battery supplies QV = CV² while charging a capacitor: half is stored, the other half is lost as heat in the resistance, whatever its value
  • energy in series: U ∝ 1/C; in parallel: U ∝ C

How to apply. Pick the form of U that contains the quantity that stays constant (V for a battery, Q for an isolated capacitor). The loss of energy on charging is always ½CV².

Example. 4 μF at 200 V: U = ½ × 4 × 10⁻⁶ × 200² = 0.08 J, the battery gave 0.16 J and 0.08 J was lost as heat. A field of 10⁵ V/m in air stores u = ½ × 8.85 × 10⁻¹² × 10¹⁰ = 0.0443 J/m³.

Dielectric between the plates

  • C = KC₀ (K = ε/ε₀ > 1)
  • with Q constant (battery removed): V = (V₀)/(K), E = (E₀)/(K), U = (U₀)/(K)
  • with V constant (battery connected): Q = KQ₀, E = E₀, U = KU₀
  • induced charge on the dielectric surface Q′ = Q(1 − (1)/(K))
  • for a conductor K → ∞ and E = 0 inside
  • breakdown: V(max) = E(max) d, dielectric strength E(max)
  • polarisation reduces the field inside

How to apply. First decide what is held constant: Q (battery off) or V (battery on). Then change C by the factor K and find Q, V, E and U from Q = CV.

Example. An air capacitor charged to 300 V and disconnected is filled with oil, K = 3: V = 100 V and E falls to 1/3. With Q = 3 μC the induced charge is Q′ = 3 × (1 − 1/3) = 2 μC.

Battery connected or disconnected

  • battery connected: V constant; Q = CV ∝ C; U = ½CV² ∝ C; E = V/d; work done by the battery = VΔQ = V²ΔC; ΔU = ½V²ΔC, so half the battery’s work goes to the field and the other half to mechanical work
  • battery disconnected: Q constant; V = Q/C ∝ 1/C; U = Q²/2C ∝ 1/C; E = σ/ε₀ does not change with d
  • work done by an external agent = ΔU (disconnected)

How to apply. Write which of Q and V stays fixed, then express U, E and the work in terms of the changing C. Energy balance: W(battery) = ΔU + W(mechanical).

Example. C₀ = 10 μF at 100 V: U₀ = 0.05 J. Fill with K = 4 with the battery on: Q = 4 mC, U = 0.2 J, the battery gives 3 × 10⁻³ × 100 = 0.3 J, so 0.15 J is mechanical work. Battery off: U = 0.05 ÷ 4 = 0.0125 J.

Dielectric or conducting slab between the plates

  • slab of thickness t and constant K in a gap d (plate area A): C = (ε₀A)/(d − t + t/K)
  • conducting slab (K → ∞): C = (ε₀A)/(d − t), independent of the position of the slab
  • slab fills the gap: C = (Kε₀A)/(d)
  • for constant Q: V = E₀(d − t) + (E₀t)/(K)
  • the slab can be shifted between the plates without changing C

How to apply. Treat the layers (air, slab, air) as capacitors in series, or equivalently take the effective gap d − t + t/K. A thin metal sheet of thickness t reduces the gap by t.

Example. A = 0.01 m², d = 5 mm, t = 3 mm of K = 3: d − t + t/K = 2 + 1 = 3 mm, so C = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² × 0.01 ÷ 0.003 = 29.5 pF. With a metal slab: 2 mm gives 44.3 pF.

Layers and side-by-side dielectrics

  • layers of thickness dᵢ and constants Kᵢ across the full area (series): C = (ε₀A)/(Σ dᵢ/Kᵢ)
  • regions of area Aᵢ side by side (parallel): C = (ε₀ΣKᵢAᵢ)/(d)
  • slab of constant K pushed a length x into plates of length L and width w: C = (ε₀w(L + (K − 1)x))/(d)
  • force pulling the slab in (battery on): F = ½V² (dC)/(dx) = (ε₀w(K − 1)V²)/(2d) (independent of x)
  • the field in layer i is E₀/Kᵢ if the same flux density passes through every layer

How to apply. Decide if the dielectrics are stacked along the field (add dᵢ/Kᵢ) or side by side (add Kᵢ Aᵢ/d). Mixed cases are series and parallel combinations of the pieces.

Example. A = 0.01 m²; layers 2 mm (K = 2) and 3 mm (K = 6): Σd/K = 1 + 0.5 = 1.5 mm, C = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² × 0.01 ÷ (1.5 × 10⁻³) = 59 pF. Two halves with K = 2 and K = 4 side by side, d = 1 mm: C = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² × (2 × 0.005 + 4 × 0.005) ÷ 10⁻³ = 265 pF.

Force between the plates

  • F = (Q²)/(2ε₀A) = ½QE = ½ε₀AE² = (ε₀AV²)/(2d²)
  • each plate feels the field of the other plate, (σ)/(2ε₀), which is half the field between them
  • pressure on the plates (σ²)/(2ε₀) = ½ε₀E²
  • work to increase the separation by Δd with Q constant: W = (Q²Δd)/(2ε₀A)
  • with the battery connected F ∝ 1/d²

How to apply. Use half of the field between the plates when finding the force on one plate. Then work = force × displacement for small changes, or the energy change for large ones.

Example. A = 0.01 m², d = 1 mm, V = 1000 V: E = 10⁶ V/m, F = ½ × 8.85 × 10⁻¹² × 0.01 × (10⁶)² = 0.0443 N.

Joining, charging and circuits

Joining charged capacitors and the heat loss

  • plates of the same sign joined: V = (C₁V₁ + C₂V₂)/(C₁ + C₂)
  • opposite plates joined: V = (C₁V₁ − C₂V₂)/(C₁ + C₂) (sign of the larger charge)
  • loss of energy (same polarity) ΔU = (C₁C₂(V₁ − V₂)²)/(2(C₁ + C₂)); opposite polarity (C₁C₂(V₁ + V₂)²)/(2(C₁ + C₂))
  • a charged capacitor joined to an uncharged one of equal C loses half its energy
  • charge is conserved; energy goes as heat and radiation, independent of the resistance

How to apply. Conserve the charge on the joined plates, put the two capacitors at the same final V, and find the energy before and after. Take care of the sign for opposite connection.

Example. 2 μF at 100 V joined to 3 μF at 50 V (same polarity): V = (200 + 150) ÷ 5 = 70 V; loss = 2 × 10⁻⁶ × 3 × 10⁻⁶ × 50² ÷ (2 × 5 × 10⁻⁶) = 1.5 × 10⁻³ J. With opposite polarity V = (200 − 150) ÷ 5 = 10 V.

Charging and discharging through a resistor

  • charging: q = CV(1 − e^−t/RC), I = (V)/(R) e^−t/RC, V(C) = V(1 − e^−t/RC)
  • discharging: q = Q₀e^−t/RC, I = I₀e^−t/RC
  • time constant τ = RC: at t = τ the charging capacitor has 63% of its final charge, a discharging one 37%
  • half-life t½ = RC ln 2 = 0.693 RC
  • current is greatest at t = 0
  • energy dissipated while charging = ½CV²

How to apply. At t = 0 an uncharged capacitor behaves as a wire, and after a long time every capacitor branch carries no current. For other times use the exponentials with τ = RC.

Example. R = 1 kΩ, C = 100 μF, V = 10 V: τ = 0.1 s, final charge 1 mC. At t = 0.1 s: q = 1 × (1 − e⁻¹) = 0.632 mC and I = (10 ÷ 1000) × e⁻¹ = 3.68 mA.

Steady-state circuits with capacitors

  • in a steady state no current flows in a branch with a capacitor: remove it, solve the resistor circuit and take V(C) as the potential difference between the capacitor’s end points
  • a capacitor in parallel with a resistor carries that resistor’s voltage
  • just after a switch is closed an uncharged capacitor is a wire; a charged one is a battery of emf Q/C
  • when a switch changes the connections, apply conservation of charge on each isolated pair of plates
  • energy supplied by a source = emf × charge passed

How to apply. Redraw the circuit with the capacitors removed, find the currents with Ohm’s law, then put the voltages across the capacitors from the potentials at their ends. Q = CV for each capacitor.

Example. 10 V battery with 3 Ω and 2 Ω in series; a 5 μF capacitor across the 2 Ω resistor: I = 10 ÷ 5 = 2 A, V(C) = 4 V, Q = 5 × 4 = 20 μC.

Several charges are assembled, moved or released. Find the energy of the arrangement, the work needed, or the speed gained. 1. U = Σ kqᵢqⱼ/rᵢⱼ over all pairs. 2. Work by the agent = ΔU = qΔV. 3. Conserve energy (and momentum for free particles). Example: Four 2 μC charges on a square of 0.3 m side: U = 0.650 J.

How do I solve “Equipotentials, E from V and graphs” questions in JEE Main?

V(x, y, z) or a V–x or E–x graph is given. Find E, the direction of the field, or V at some point. 1. E = −dV/dx for each coordinate. 2. Read slope for E and area for ΔV. 3. E is perpendicular to the equipotential, towards lower V. Example: V = 2x² + 3y: at x = 1, E = (−4, −3), |E| = 5 V/m.

How do I solve “Conducting spheres, shells and charge sharing” questions in JEE Main?

Spheres or shells are connected by wires, earthed, or have charges inside cavities. Find charges, potentials or the heat lost. 1. Use conservation of charge and equal potential. 2. Induced charges: −q inside, +q outside the shell. 3. Earthing: set V = 0 to find the charge. Example: 6 μC on a 0.1 m sphere joined to an uncharged 0.2 m sphere: 2 μC and 4 μC; 1.08 J lost.

How do I solve “Capacitance of geometry and combinations” questions in JEE Main?

A capacitor of a given shape or a network of capacitors is given. Find the capacitance between two points, the charge or the voltage on one of them. 1. Parallel plate ε₀A/d, sphere 4πε₀R, spherical 4πε₀ab/(b − a). 2. Simplify series and parallel groups step by step. 3. Q is the same in series and V is the same in parallel. Example: 2 μF and 3 μF in series across 100 V: 60 V and 40 V.

How do I solve “Dielectric slab, conducting slab and layers” questions in JEE Main?

A slab or several layers fill the gap of a parallel plate capacitor. Find C, the field, the charge or the energy, with the battery on or off. 1. C = ε₀A/(d − t + t/K); layers: Σdᵢ/Kᵢ. 2. Say what stays constant: V (battery on) or Q (battery off). 3. Find the energy and the work done on the slab. Example: A = 0.01 m², d = 5 mm, slab 3 mm of K = 3: C = 29.5 pF.

How do I solve “Energy, force and heat loss in capacitors” questions in JEE Main?

A capacitor is charged, its plates are pulled apart, or two capacitors are joined. Find the energy stored, the force or the heat produced. 1. U = ½CV² or Q²/2C, as the case requires. 2. Force between plates Q²/2ε₀A. 3. Joined: conserve charge and find the common V. Example: 2 μF at 100 V joined to 3 μF at 50 V: V = 70 V, loss 1.5 mJ.

How do I solve “Charging, discharging and steady-state circuits” questions in JEE Main?

A capacitor is in a circuit with resistors. Find the charge or current at a time, or the charge once a steady state has been reached. 1. Steady state: no current through the capacitor. 2. Voltage across it = potential difference of its ends. 3. Time dependence: q = Q(1 − e^−t/RC) or Qe^−t/RC. Example: R = 1 kΩ, C = 100 μF, 10 V: at t = RC the charge is 0.632 mC.

Common myths and the truth

  • Myth: The field is zero where the potential is zero. Truth: On the equatorial plane of a dipole the potential is zero but the field is not; at the centre of a ring the field is zero but the potential is not.
  • Myth: The flux through a closed surface depends on the charges outside. Truth: The net flux depends only on the charge inside; outside charges add as much flux going in as going out.
  • Myth: Gauss’s law gives the field of any charge distribution. Truth: It is useful only when the field has a symmetry that makes E constant on the surface; it is always true, but not always easy.
  • Myth: A charge released from rest always moves along a field line. Truth: It follows a field line only if the line is straight; otherwise its velocity and the line are different.
  • Myth: The electric field inside a charged shell is zero because there is no charge in it. Truth: Zero field inside comes from symmetry: the field of the shell charge cancels at every point of the hollow.
  • Myth: A dielectric always lowers the energy of a capacitor. Truth: With the battery connected the energy goes up by the factor K; it goes down only when the charge is fixed.
  • Myth: Energy is lost only when there is a resistor of large value. Truth: Half the battery’s energy is lost in charging whatever the resistance; it is the heat produced by the current.
  • Myth: The capacitance of a capacitor depends on its charge and voltage. Truth: It depends only on the geometry and the medium; Q and V change together in C = Q/V.
  • Myth: A conducting slab in a capacitor has no effect on the capacitance. Truth: It shortens the gap by its thickness t (C = ε₀A/(d − t)), though the position of the slab does not matter.
  • Myth: In series capacitors the voltage divides equally. Truth: The charge is equal; the voltage is larger across the smaller capacitor (V ∝ 1/C).

Mistakes to avoid

  • A field is a vector: add components, not magnitudes.
  • Potential is a scalar: add with signs; the field can be zero where the potential is not zero, and the other way round.
  • The field inside a shell is zero, the potential is not.
  • The energy of a system counts each pair once; do not double it.
  • In a medium, divide Coulomb’s force and the field by K; capacitance is multiplied by K.
  • With the battery connected V is constant; with the battery removed Q is constant.
  • The force on a plate uses half of the field between the plates.
  • In RC charging the exponent is −t/RC; use 1 − e^−t/RC for charge on a charging capacitor.
