Optics: JEE Main Physics formulas and notes

Optics is one of the biggest chapters in JEE Mains Physics: ray optics (mirrors, refraction, TIR, lenses, prism, instruments) and wave optics (Huygens, YDSE, diffraction, polarisation) together give 3 to 4 questions every year. The formulas are many but standard; most marks are lost to sign errors, using the wrong focal length in a liquid, or confusing the fringe width with the position of a fringe. About 20 hours is enough.

Quick revision

  • Mirror: 1/v + 1/u = 1/f = 2/R; m = −v/u; concave f < 0
  • Snell n₁ sin i = n₂ sin r; sinθc = n₂/n₁; apparent depth d/n; shift t(1 − 1/n)
  • Surface: n₂/v − n₁/u = (n₂ − n₁)/R
  • Lens: 1/v − 1/u = 1/f = (n − 1)(1/R₁ − 1/R₂); P = 1/f; m = v/u
  • Contact P = P₁ + P₂; separated 1/f = 1/f₁ + 1/f₂ − d/f₁f₂
  • Prism: δ = i + e − A; n = sin((A + δₘ)/2)/sin(A/2); thin δ = (n − 1)A; ω = (nᵥ − nᵣ)/(n − 1)
  • Microscope M = (v₀/u₀)(1 + D/fₑ); telescope M = −f₀/fₑ, L = f₀ + fₑ
  • Myopia f = −x; hypermetropia 1/f = 1/25 − 1/d; resolving 1.22λ/D
  • I = I₁ + I₂ + 2√(I₁I₂) cosφ; YDSE β = λD/d; shift (n − 1)tD/d
  • Single slit a sinθ = nλ, central width 2λD/a; Malus I₀cos²θ; Brewster tan i = n; film 2nt = (m + ½)λ

Formulas with solved examples

Reflection and mirrors

Plane mirror

  • laws: angle of incidence = angle of reflection; incident ray, reflected ray and normal in one plane
  • image: virtual, erect, same size, as far behind as the object is in front
  • a mirror turned through θ turns the reflected ray through 2θ (incident ray fixed)
  • two mirrors at angle θ: number of images n = (360)/(θ) − 1 if 360/θ is even; for odd 360/θ, n = (360)/(θ) − 1 for an object on the bisector and 360/θ otherwise
  • a person sees the full image in a mirror of half his height
  • object speed v: image speed v (relative to the mirror), closing speed 2v if the mirror is fixed

How to apply. Draw the image behind the mirror at equal distance. For rotation use 2θ. For two mirrors find 360/θ first, then subtract 1 when it is even.

Example. Mirrors at 60°: n = 360 ÷ 60 − 1 = 5 images. A mirror turned by 15° turns the reflected ray by 30°. A person walks towards a fixed mirror at 2 m/s: the image approaches him at 4 m/s.

Spherical mirror formula and sign convention

  • (1)/(v) + (1)/(u) = (1)/(f) = (2)/(R), f = (R)/(2)
  • m = (h′)/(h) = −(v)/(u) = (f)/(f − u)
  • Cartesian convention: pole at the origin, incident light travels along +x; distances against it are negative
  • concave mirror: f < 0 (focus in front); convex mirror: f > 0
  • real image: v < 0 (in front of the mirror)
  • m < 0 inverted, m > 0 erect

How to apply. Write u (always negative for a real object), f with its sign (negative for concave, positive for convex), then find 1/v = 1/f − 1/u. Use m = −v/u to find size and nature.

Example. Concave mirror f = −20 cm, object at u = −30 cm: 1/v = −1/20 + 1/30 = −1/60, v = −60 cm (real); m = −(−60)/(−30) = −2: inverted, twice as large. Convex mirror f = +15 cm, u = −30 cm: 1/v = 1/15 + 1/30, v = +10 cm (virtual), m = +1/3.

Magnification and the moving object

  • for an object moving along the axis: v(image) = −m² v(object) along the axis (dv/du = −m²)
  • lateral magnification m = f/(f − u) for a mirror
  • concave mirror image at the centre of curvature has m = −1
  • Newton’s relation x₁x₂ = f², x measured from the focus
  • an object between P and F of a concave mirror gives a virtual, magnified, erect image

How to apply. Find m at the given position, then speed of image = m² × speed of object, in the same direction as the object’s speed (for mirrors the image moves the same way along x).

Example. Concave f = −20 cm, object at 30 cm so m = −2; object moves at 2 cm/s towards the mirror: image speed = 4 × 2 = 8 cm/s (away from the mirror, since v = −60 cm grows in size).

Refraction and total internal reflection

Snell’s law and speed of light in a medium

  • n₁ sinθ₁ = n₂ sinθ₂
  • n = (c)/(v) = (λ₀)/(λ)
  • (sin i)/(sin r) = (v₁)/(v₂) = (λ₁)/(λ₂) = (n₂)/(n₁)
  • frequency does not change on refraction
  • the ray bends towards the normal on entering a denser medium
  • relative index n₂₁ = n₂/n₁

How to apply. Measure both angles from the normal. Apply n₁ sin i = n₂ sin r, then v = c/n and λ = λ₀/n if speed or wavelength is asked.

Example. Light from air at 60° enters glass n = 1.5: sin r = 0.866 ÷ 1.5 = 0.577, r = 35.3°. In glass v = 2 × 10⁸ m/s and a 600 nm wave has λ = 400 nm.

Apparent depth and slab

  • object in a denser medium of index n seen from the rarer side (near the normal): apparent depth = (real depth)/(n)
  • in general d(app) = d (n(observer))/(n(object))
  • shift of an object seen through a slab of thickness t: Δ = t(1 − (1)/(n)) towards the observer
  • several layers: apparent depth = Σ (tᵢ)/(nᵢ)
  • apparent position of the bottom of a vessel rises by d(1 − 1/n)

How to apply. Divide each real thickness by its index (for an observer in air) and add. The shift is t(1 − 1/n), directed towards the observer.

Example. A fish 24 cm below the surface of water (n = 4/3) appears at 18 cm. A coin under a 6 cm glass slab (n = 1.5) looks raised by 6(1 − 2/3) = 2 cm.

Lateral shift in a glass slab

  • lateral shift d = (t sin(i − r))/(cos r)
  • emergent ray is parallel to the incident ray
  • for small angles d ≈ t i (1 − 1/n)
  • the shift is zero at normal incidence
  • it is greatest (equal to t) for grazing incidence if n is very large

How to apply. Find r from Snell’s law, then compute sin(i − r) and cos r.

Example. Slab t = 3 cm, n = 1.5, i = 45°: sin r = 0.7071 ÷ 1.5 = 0.471, r = 28.1°; d = 3 × sin(16.9°) ÷ cos 28.1° = 3 × 0.290 ÷ 0.882 = 0.99 cm.

Total internal reflection and critical angle

  • light going from denser (n₁) to rarer (n₂) medium: sinθc = (n₂)/(n₁) (rarer medium air: sinθc = 1/n)
  • TIR for angle of incidence i > θc
  • at i = θc the refracted ray grazes the surface
  • circle of light from a source at depth h under a liquid: R = h tanθc = (h)/(√(n² − 1))
  • uses: diamond (θc = 24.4°), optical fibre, prisms in binoculars, mirage

How to apply. Check that the ray goes from a higher to a lower index. Compare the incident angle with θc. For a source below a surface, the light that escapes fills a circle of radius R.

Example. Water n = 4/3: sinθc = 0.75, θc = 48.6°. A lamp 7 cm deep gives a bright circle of radius R = 7 ÷ √(16/9 − 1) = 7.94 cm. Glass n = 1.5: θc = 41.8°.

Optical fibre

  • core index n₁ and cladding n₂ < n₁
  • light is guided by TIR if the angle at the core–cladding surface exceeds θc
  • numerical aperture NA = sin θ(max) = √(n₁² − n₂²) (light entering from air)
  • acceptance angle θ(max) = sin⁻¹√(n₁² − n₂²)

How to apply. Compute n₁² − n₂² and take the square root; the inverse sine is the largest angle of entry measured from the axis.

Example. n₁ = 1.5, n₂ = 1.4: NA = √(2.25 − 1.96) = 0.539, θ(max) = 32.6°.

Refraction at a spherical surface

  • (n₂)/(v) − (n₁)/(u) = (n₂ − n₁)/(R)
  • light travels from n₁ to n₂ along +x; R is positive if the centre of curvature lies on the side where light goes (convex towards the object)
  • lateral magnification m = (n₁v)/(n₂u)
  • f₁ = (n₁R)/(n₂ − n₁) (object side), f₂ = (n₂R)/(n₂ − n₁)

How to apply. Fix signs: u negative, R positive for a surface convex towards the object. Substitute into n₂/v − n₁/u = (n₂ − n₁)/R and solve for v.

Example. Air to glass (n = 1.5), R = +15 cm, object at u = −45 cm: 1.5/v = 0.5/15 + 1/(−45) = 1/30 − 1/45 = 1/90, v = +135 cm (real image). For u = −30 cm the image is at infinity.

Lenses

Thin lens formula and lens maker’s formula

  • (1)/(v) − (1)/(u) = (1)/(f)
  • (1)/(f) = (n − 1)((1)/(R₁) − (1)/(R₂)) with n = n(lens)/n(medium)
  • m = (v)/(u) = (h′)/(h)
  • power P = (1)/(f) (f in metre, unit dioptre); convex lens f > 0, concave f < 0
  • for a lens in a liquid replace n by n(lens)/n(liquid); f increases (a lens with n = n(liquid) vanishes)

How to apply. Take the first surface the light meets as R₁. Use signs: R positive when the centre is on the outgoing side. Get f from the maker’s formula, then use 1/v = 1/f + 1/u.

Example. Biconvex lens R₁ = +20 cm, R₂ = −20 cm, n = 1.5: 1/f = 0.5 × (1/20 + 1/20) = 0.05, f = 20 cm, P = +5 D. In water (4/3): n = 1.125 and 1/f = 0.125 × 0.1, f = 80 cm. For u = −30 cm in air: 1/v = 1/20 − 1/30, v = 60 cm, m = −2.

Image positions and the minimum distance

  • object beyond 2f: real, inverted, diminished, image between f and 2f
  • at 2f: real, inverted, same size, image at 2f
  • between f and 2f: real, inverted, magnified, image beyond 2f
  • at f: image at infinity
  • inside f: virtual, erect, magnified
  • minimum distance between a real object and its real image is 4f (at u = v = 2f)
  • Newton’s formula: x₁x₂ = f²

How to apply. Place the object relative to f and 2f to name the image. For object–screen distance D > 4f there are two lens positions.

Example. f = 20 cm and the object at 40 cm gives a real inverted image of the same size at 40 cm, 80 cm = 4f from the object. An object at 15 cm gives 1/v = 1/20 − 1/15, v = −60 cm: virtual, m = +4.

Combinations of lenses

  • lenses in contact: P = P₁ + P₂, (1)/(f) = (1)/(f₁) + (1)/(f₂)
  • separated by d: (1)/(f) = (1)/(f₁) + (1)/(f₂) − (d)/(f₁f₂)
  • for a series of lenses, the image of one is the object of the next; total m = m₁ × m₂
  • a thin lens and a mirror: the equivalent mirror has P = 2P(lens) + P(mirror) if light passes the lens twice

How to apply. Add powers directly if the lenses touch. Otherwise take the image of the first lens as the object of the second, or use the d formula for the equivalent focal length.

Example. +10 cm and −20 cm in contact: 1/f = 0.1 − 0.05 = 0.05, f = +20 cm, P = 10 − 5 = +5 D. Two lenses of f = 20 cm separated by 10 cm: 1/f = 1/20 + 1/20 − 10/400 = 0.075, f = 13.3 cm.

Cut lenses, silvered lens and displacement method

  • cut a symmetric lens in two along a plane containing the axis (two halves side by side): f unchanged, image fainter
  • cut perpendicular to the axis (two plano-convex halves): f doubles for each half
  • plano-convex lens silvered on the curved or plane face behaves as a mirror: (1)/(F) = (2)/(f(lens)) + (1)/(f(mirror))
  • displacement method (object–screen distance D, lens positions d apart): f = (D² − d²)/(4D), and the size of the object h = √(h₁h₂)

How to apply. Decide whether the lens is cut along or across the axis. For a silvered lens add the powers: twice for the lens and once for the mirror surface.

Example. Displacement method: D = 90 cm, d = 30 cm: f = (8100 − 900) ÷ 360 = 20 cm. A symmetric biconvex lens of f = 20 cm cut across its thickness gives two plano-convex lenses of f = 40 cm each.

Prism and dispersion

Prism: deviation and refractive index

  • δ = i + e − A
  • r₁ + r₂ = A
  • at minimum deviation i = e and r₁ = r₂ = (A)/(2): n = (sin((A + δₘ)/2))/(sin(A/2))
  • δₘ is the smallest deviation; the i–δ graph is U-shaped
  • for a thin prism (small A) δ = (n − 1)A
  • normal incidence on the first face: r₁ = 0, r₂ = A, sin e = n sin A, δ = e − A

How to apply. Draw the ray; use r₁ + r₂ = A inside. At minimum deviation take i = (A + δₘ)/2. Check whether the ray emerges (r₂ < θc).

Example. A = 60°, δₘ = 30°: n = sin 45° ÷ sin 30° = 1.414. Thin prism A = 4°, n = 1.5: δ = 2°. Normal incidence, A = 30°, n = √2: sin e = 0.707, e = 45°, δ = 15°.

Prism: limits of emergence

  • for emergence r₂ ≤ θc, so r₁ ≥ A − θc
  • prism angle limit for an emergent ray with any incidence: A ≤ 2θc
  • maximum angle for a ray normally incident on one face to emerge from the other: A < θc
  • for a prism in a liquid use the relative index n(prism)/n(liquid)

How to apply. Compute θc = sin⁻¹(1/n) and compare with the angle r₂ (or A, for normal incidence).

Example. n = 1.5 (θc = 41.8°): a ray can emerge from a prism only if A ≤ 83.6°; for normal incidence the ray emerges from the second face only if A < 41.8°.

Dispersion and dispersive power

  • n = a + (b)/(λ²) (Cauchy): n is greater for violet than red
  • angular dispersion θ = δᵥ − δᵣ = (nᵥ − nᵣ)A (thin prism)
  • mean deviation δ = (n − 1)A with n = (nᵥ + nᵣ)/2
  • dispersive power ω = (nᵥ − nᵣ)/(n − 1) = (θ)/(δ)
  • achromatic combination of two thin prisms: (nᵥ − nᵣ)A = (nᵥ′ − nᵣ′)A′ (no net dispersion), prisms placed opposite
  • direct vision: net deviation zero, δ − δ′ = 0

How to apply. For a single prism find δ for each colour and subtract. For an achromatic pair equate the angular dispersions of the two prisms; the deviation left over is (n − 1)A − (n′ − 1)A′.

Example. nᵣ = 1.51, nᵥ = 1.53, A = 5°: angular dispersion = 0.02 × 5 = 0.1°; n = 1.52, ω = 0.02 ÷ 0.52 = 0.038. Crown (A = 10°, nᵥ − nᵣ = 0.016, n = 1.52) with flint (nᵥ′ − nᵣ′ = 0.032, n′ = 1.65): A′ = 5° removes dispersion; net deviation 5.2° − 3.25° = 1.95°.

Scattering and natural phenomena

  • Rayleigh scattering intensity ∝ (1)/(λ⁴): blue and violet scatter more than red (blue sky, red sunrise and sunset)
  • rainbow: primary bow by one internal reflection in a raindrop, red outside at about 42°, violet inside at 40°
  • secondary bow (two internal reflections) is fainter with reversed colours
  • twinkling: varying refraction in the atmosphere
  • mirage: TIR in a hot air layer near the ground
  • the Sun is visible about 2 minutes before it rises

How to apply. Compare wavelengths and raise the ratio to the fourth power. For a bow, the observer’s back is towards the Sun.

Example. Violet (400 nm) versus red (700 nm): (700/400)⁴ = 9.4, so violet is scattered about 9.4 times more.

Optical instruments and the eye

Simple microscope (magnifier)

  • M = (θ′)/(θ)
  • image at the near point (D = 25 cm): M = 1 + (D)/(f)
  • image at infinity: M = (D)/(f)
  • the object is placed at or just inside the focus
  • a lens of short focal length magnifies more

How to apply. Put the image where asked: near point gives 1 + D/f, infinity gives D/f. D = 25 cm unless given otherwise.

Example. f = 5 cm: M = 1 + 25/5 = 6 at the near point and 5 at infinity.

Compound microscope

  • objective (short f₀) forms a real magnified image, the eyepiece (fₑ) acts as a magnifier
  • M = m₀ × Mₑ = (v₀)/(|u₀|)(1 + (D)/(fₑ)) for the image at D
  • M = (v₀)/(|u₀|)((D)/(fₑ)) for the image at infinity
  • approximately M ≈ (L)/(f₀)(D)/(fₑ) (L = tube length between the focal points)
  • tube length = v₀ + uₑ

How to apply. Find v₀ from the lens formula for the objective, m₀ = v₀/u₀, then Mₑ for the final position. Multiply.

Example. f₀ = 1 cm, fₑ = 5 cm, object at 1.1 cm: 1/v₀ = 1 − 1/1.1, v₀ = 11 cm, m₀ = −10. Image at infinity: M = 10 × 25/5 = 50 (tube length 11 + 5 = 16 cm). Image at 25 cm: M = 10 × 6 = 60.

Astronomical telescope

  • objective of large f₀, eyepiece fₑ
  • normal adjustment (final image at infinity): M = −(f₀)/(fₑ), length L = f₀ + fₑ
  • final image at D: M = −(f₀)/(fₑ)(1 + (fₑ)/(D)), L = f₀ + uₑ with uₑ = (Dfₑ)/(D + fₑ)
  • aperture D₀: light gathering ∝ D₀², resolving power ∝ D₀
  • inverted image

How to apply. Use L = f₀ + fₑ for normal adjustment. For near-point viewing find the eyepiece distance uₑ from the lens formula with v = −D.

Example. f₀ = 100 cm, fₑ = 5 cm: M = −20 and L = 105 cm. Final image at 25 cm: M = −20 × 1.2 = −24, uₑ = 125 ÷ 30 = 4.17 cm, L = 104.2 cm.

Terrestrial, Galilean and reflecting telescopes

  • terrestrial telescope: an extra erecting lens, M = (f₀)/(fₑ), length f₀ + fₑ + 4f
  • Galilean telescope: concave eyepiece, M = (f₀)/(fₑ), L = f₀ − fₑ, erect image
  • reflecting telescope (Cassegrain): concave mirror objective, no chromatic aberration, larger aperture, and it is easier to make and support

How to apply. For the Galilean telescope subtract the focal lengths: the eyepiece is a concave lens placed inside the focus of the objective.

Example. Galilean telescope with f₀ = 30 cm and fₑ = 3 cm: M = 10 and L = 27 cm.

Resolving power

  • telescope: smallest angle θ = (1.22λ)/(D₀), resolving power = (D₀)/(1.22λ)
  • microscope: smallest distance d = (1.22λ)/(2n sinβ) (n sinβ the numerical aperture), R.P. = (2n sinβ)/(1.22λ) (or d = λ/2n sinβ in the simpler form)
  • greater aperture and shorter λ resolve better
  • the eye (pupil 2 mm) resolves about 3 × 10⁻⁴ rad
  • the circular aperture also controls diffraction blur

How to apply. Apply the right formula to the instrument: use the diameter D₀ for a telescope and the numerical aperture for a microscope. Keep λ in metre.

Example. A telescope with a 5 m mirror at λ = 550 nm: θ = 1.22 × 550 × 10⁻⁹ ÷ 5 = 1.34 × 10⁻⁷ rad. Microscope with n sinβ = 0.8 and λ = 600 nm: d = 1.22 × 600 × 10⁻⁹ ÷ 1.6 = 4.6 × 10⁻⁷ m. Eye of pupil 2 mm, λ = 500 nm: θ = 3.05 × 10⁻⁴ rad.

Defects of vision

  • myopia (short sight): far point at x (< infinity): a concave lens with f = −x, P = −(1)/(x)
  • hypermetropia (long sight): near point at d > 25 cm: convex lens with (1)/(f) = (1)/(25) − (1)/(d), f = (25d)/(d − 25)
  • presbyopia: bifocal lenses
  • astigmatism: cylindrical lens
  • normal eye: near point 25 cm, far point at infinity

How to apply. Place the object where it should be seen (infinity for myopia, 25 cm for hypermetropia) and the image at the actual far or near point. Then use the lens formula 1/f = 1/v − 1/u.

Example. Far point 80 cm: f = −80 cm, P = −1.25 D. Near point 50 cm: f = 25 × 50 ÷ 25 = 50 cm, P = +2 D (check: 1/v − 1/u = −1/50 + 1/25 = 1/50).

Wavefronts and interference

Huygens’ principle, wavefronts and optical path

  • every point of a wavefront is a source of secondary wavelets; the new wavefront is the forward envelope
  • point source gives spherical, a distant source plane wavefronts, a lens makes them spherical (converging or diverging)
  • reflection and refraction follow from Huygens: i = r, (sin i)/(sin r) = (v₁)/(v₂)
  • optical path = n x (the distance light travels in vacuum in the same time)
  • phase difference φ = (2π)/(λ) × (path difference)

How to apply. Convert every path to optical path (n x) and then to phase using φ = 2π Δ/λ. A glass plate of thickness t adds (n − 1)t to the path.

Example. A glass sheet n = 1.5 and t = 3 μm in a path adds (0.5)(3 μm) = 1.5 μm. For λ = 600 nm that is 2.5 wavelengths, a phase difference 5π.

Superposition and intensity of two waves

  • I = I₁ + I₂ + 2√(I₁I₂) cosφ
  • I(max) = (√I₁ + √I₂)², I(min) = (√I₁ − √I₂)²
  • equal sources: I = 4I₀cos²(φ)/(2)
  • (I(max))/(I(min)) = ((a + 1)/(a − 1))² with a = A₁/A₂
  • I ∝ A²; I₁ : I₂ = w₁ : w₂ for slit widths
  • maximum when Δ = nλ (φ = 2nπ), minimum when Δ = (n + ½)λ
  • sources must be coherent (same frequency, fixed phase difference); two bulbs are not

How to apply. Convert path difference to phase: φ = 2πΔ/λ. Use the general formula for unequal sources or 4I₀cos²(φ/2) for equal ones. For a ratio of I use a = √(I₁/I₂).

Example. I₁ : I₂ = 9 : 1: a = 3, I(max)/I(min) = (4/2)² = 4. Two equal sources I₀ with φ = 60°: I = 4I₀cos²30° = 3I₀.

Young’s double slit: fringes

  • path difference Δ = d sinθ ≈ (dy)/(D)
  • bright fringes y = (nλD)/(d) (n = 0, ±1, …), dark y = (n + ½)(λD)/(d)
  • fringe width β = (λD)/(d), angular fringe width λ/d
  • fringes are equally spaced; β ∝ λ, D and 1/d
  • in a medium n: β′ = β/n
  • I = 4I₀cos²((πdy)/(λD))
  • valid for d ≪ D

How to apply. Write Δ = dy/D and set it equal to nλ (bright) or (n + ½)λ (dark). Fringe width is the distance between two neighbouring bright fringes.

Example. λ = 600 nm, D = 1.5 m, d = 0.3 mm: β = 6 × 10⁻⁷ × 1.5 ÷ 3 × 10⁻⁴ = 3 mm. The 4th bright fringe is at 12 mm. Under water (4/3) β = 2.25 mm.

Shift of the fringes by a thin sheet

  • a transparent sheet of thickness t and index n in front of one slit adds a path (n − 1)t
  • the whole pattern shifts towards the covered slit by Δy = ((n − 1)tD)/(d) = ((n − 1)t)/(λ) β
  • number of fringes shifted N = ((n − 1)t)/(λ)
  • the fringe width is unchanged

How to apply. Find the extra optical path (n − 1)t, divide by λ to get the number of fringes N, and multiply by β to get the shift.

Example. Sheet n = 1.5, t = 6 μm, λ = 600 nm, β = 3 mm: N = 0.5 × 6 × 10⁻⁶ ÷ 6 × 10⁻⁷ = 5 fringes, shift = 5 × 3 = 15 mm toward the covered slit.

Two wavelengths and white light

  • two wavelengths λ₁ and λ₂: bright fringes coincide where n₁λ₁ = n₂λ₂
  • white light: central fringe white, then coloured fringes (violet nearest), the pattern disappears after a few
  • for a missing wavelength at a point, set the path difference (n + ½)λ
  • fringe width ∝ λ so β(red) > β(violet)
  • source slit moved by s at distance L from the slits shifts the pattern by sD/L on the other side

How to apply. Find the smallest integers n₁ : n₂ = λ₂ : λ₁. The distance of the first coincidence is n₁β₁.

Example. λ₁ = 600 nm, λ₂ = 500 nm: n₁ : n₂ = 5 : 6; the first coincidence is at the 5th fringe of 600 nm (the 6th of 500 nm), y = 5 × 3 mm = 15 mm.

Diffraction and resolution

Single slit diffraction

  • minima: a sinθ = nλ (n = ±1, ±2, …)
  • maxima approximately a sinθ = (n + ½)λ
  • width of central maximum 2λD/a (angular width 2λ/a); other maxima have width λD/a
  • I = I₀((sin α)/(α))², α = (πa sinθ)/(λ)
  • the first secondary maximum has about 4.5% of I₀
  • diffraction is noticeable when a is comparable to λ
  • different from YDSE: fringes are unequal in brightness

How to apply. Set a sinθ = λ for the first minimum, with y = D tanθ ≈ Dθ. The central maximum extends from −y₁ to +y₁.

Example. a = 0.2 mm, λ = 500 nm, D = 1 m: first minimum at y = λD/a = 2.5 mm; width of the central maximum = 5 mm.

Double slit with finite slit width

  • the YDSE fringes lie under the single-slit diffraction envelope
  • the n-th order is missing if it falls at a single-slit minimum: d sinθ = mλ and a sinθ = kλ, so d/a = m/k
  • bright fringes in the central diffraction maximum: 2(d/a) − 1 when d/a is an integer
  • fringe spacing is λD/d, envelope width 2λD/a

How to apply. Find d/a; the order that equals this ratio is missing. The central envelope holds all orders below it on each side, plus the central one.

Example. d = 0.6 mm and a = 0.1 mm (d/a = 6): the 6th order is missing, so the central diffraction maximum has 2 × 6 − 1 = 11 bright fringes.

Polarisation

Polarisation and Malus’ law

  • only transverse waves can be polarised
  • unpolarised light through a polariser: I = (I₀)/(2), plane polarised
  • Malus’ law: I = I₀ cos²θ (θ between the transmission axes)
  • unpolarised light, polariser then analyser: I = (I₀)/(2)cos²θ
  • rotating the analyser through 360° gives two maxima and two zeros
  • polarisation methods: Polaroid (dichroism), reflection, scattering, double refraction

How to apply. The first polariser always halves unpolarised light. After that multiply by cos² of the angle between successive axes.

Example. Unpolarised I₀ through polarisers at 0°, 30° and 90°: I = (I₀/2)cos²30°cos²60° = (I₀/2)(3/4)(1/4) = 3I₀/32. With only two crossed polarisers the light is zero; a third at 45° between them gives I₀/8.

Brewster’s law

  • tan iʙ = n (light from air on a medium n)
  • at iʙ the reflected ray is completely plane polarised, with vibrations perpendicular to the plane of incidence
  • reflected and refracted rays are at 90°: iʙ + r = 90°
  • from a medium n₁ to n₂: tan iʙ = n₂/n₁
  • the refracted ray is partly polarised

How to apply. Check whether the angle is Brewster’s; use tan i = n or the fact that the reflected and refracted rays are perpendicular.

Example. Glass n = 1.5: iʙ = tan⁻¹1.5 = 56.3° and r = 33.7°. Water n = 4/3: iʙ = 53.1°.

Thin films

Thin-film interference and coatings

  • reflected light from a film of thickness t and index n in air (normal incidence): path difference 2nt, with an extra λ/2 from the phase change on reflection from the denser surface
  • bright (reflected): 2nt = (m + ½)λ
  • dark (reflected): 2nt = mλ
  • transmitted light shows the opposite
  • non-reflecting coating on glass: n(film) = √n(glass), minimum thickness t = (λ)/(4n)
  • colours of soap bubbles and oil films come from this

How to apply. Count the λ/2 phase jumps: one jump (air to film) means reflected maxima are at 2nt = (m + ½)λ. For a coating on glass both reflections jump, so the minimum for darkness is 2nt = λ/2.

Example. Soap film n = 1.33, bright in reflected light for λ = 560 nm with the smallest thickness: t = 560 ÷ (4 × 1.33) = 105 nm. A MgF₂ coating (n = 1.38) for 550 nm on glass: t = 550 ÷ (4 × 1.38) = 99.6 nm.

Light passes into a medium or an object is viewed through water or glass. Find the angle, the apparent depth, the shift or the speed. 1. Use Snell’s law with the angles from the normal. 2. Apparent depth = real depth/n. 3. Shift = t(1 − 1/n). Example: Fish 24 cm deep in water appears at 18 cm.

Light goes from a denser to a rarer medium, or a source is placed under a liquid. Find the critical angle, whether TIR occurs, or the radius of the light circle. 1. sinθc = n₂/n₁. 2. The indices for red and violet are given with the angle of a thin prism. How do I solve “Interference of two waves: intensity and phase” questions in JEE Main?

Two waves of known intensities or amplitudes meet. Find the resultant intensity, the ratio of maximum to minimum, or the phase difference. 1. I = I₁ + I₂ + 2√(I₁I₂) cosφ. 2. Imax/Imin = ((a + 1)/(a − 1))². 3. φ = 2πΔ/λ. Example: Intensity ratio 9 : 1 gives Imax/Imin = 4.

How do I solve “Young’s double slit: fringe width and position” questions in JEE Main?

The slit separation, the screen distance and the wavelength are given. Find the fringe width, the position of a bright or dark fringe, or the effect of a medium. 1. β = λD/d. 2. Bright y = nλD/d, dark (n + ½)λD/d. 3. In a medium divide β by n. Example: λ = 600 nm, D = 1.5 m, d = 0.3 mm: β = 3 mm.

How do I solve “Single slit diffraction” questions in JEE Main?

The diameter of a lens or the numerical aperture is given. Find the smallest angle or distance that can be resolved. 1. Telescope θ = 1.22λ/D. 2. Microscope d = 1.22λ/(2n sinβ). 3. Larger aperture and smaller λ resolve better. Example: 5 m mirror, 550 nm: θ = 1.34 × 10⁻⁷ rad.

How do I solve “Malus’ law and polarisers in a row” questions in JEE Main?

Unpolarised or plane polarised light falls on polarisers with axes at angles. Find the transmitted intensity or the angle needed. 1. Unpolarised through the first polariser: I₀/2. 2. Light falls on glass or water and the reflected ray is fully polarised. Find the angle of incidence, the index or the angle of refraction. 1. tan iʙ = n. 2. Reflected and refracted rays are perpendicular. 3. iʙ + r = 90°. Example: Glass n = 1.5: iʙ = 56.3°, r = 33.7°.

Common myths and the truth

  • Myth: A concave mirror always forms a real image. Truth: If the object is between the pole and the focus, the image is virtual, erect and magnified.
  • Myth: A convex lens always converges and a concave lens always diverges. Truth: A glass lens does so in air; in a liquid of higher index the behaviour is reversed.
  • Myth: The colour of light changes when it enters glass. Truth: The frequency, which sets the colour, stays the same; only the speed and wavelength change.
  • Myth: Total internal reflection can occur when light goes into a denser medium. Truth: It needs a rarer medium beyond the surface and an angle of incidence greater than the critical angle.
  • Myth: The deviation produced by a prism is smallest when the incidence angle is small. Truth: It is smallest at the symmetrical position i = e, where the ray travels parallel to the base.
  • Myth: In Young’s experiment the fringes are brighter at the centre and fade away. Truth: With narrow slits they are of nearly equal brightness; the envelope is due to single-slit diffraction.
  • Myth: Diffraction occurs only through narrow slits. Truth: It occurs at every edge and aperture; it is noticeable only when the opening is comparable to the wavelength.
  • Myth: A Polaroid removes half of any light, whichever way it is turned. Truth: It halves unpolarised light; for polarised light the output depends on the angle through Malus’ law.
  • Myth: The reflected light at Brewster’s angle is completely polarised and so is the refracted light. Truth: The reflected ray is completely polarised; the refracted ray is only partly polarised.
  • Myth: A telescope magnifies by making the image larger on the retina only. Truth: It increases the visual angle; a bigger objective also gathers more light and resolves finer detail.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Sign errors: u is negative for a real object, f is negative for a concave mirror and a concave lens.
  • The fringe width is a distance between neighbouring fringes, not the position of one.
  • Frequency does not change in a medium; the wavelength does.
  • A lens in a liquid has a longer focal length; the n in the lens maker’s formula is the ratio.
  • The first polariser reduces unpolarised light to half; then Malus’ law applies.
  • For minimum deviation, use i = e and r = A/2.
  • A sheet over one slit shifts the pattern towards that slit, by the number of fringes (n − 1)t/λ.
  • Resolving power is greater for a larger D and a smaller λ; do not invert them.

Simulations for Optics

  • Real and apparent depth — Look straight down into a pool. Refraction makes the coin on the bottom appear raised.
  • Astronomical telescope — A long-focus objective collects light from a distant object; a short-focus eyepiece magnifies the angle.
  • Compound microscope — An objective makes a magnified real image; the eyepiece magnifies it further.
  • Image in a concave mirror — Move an object in front of a concave mirror and locate its image.
  • Two thin lenses in contact — Combine converging and diverging lenses and discover their equivalent optical power.
  • Image through a convex lens — Move an object toward or away from a lens and trace the rays to its image.
  • Lens maker’s formula — Shape a lens by choosing its surface radii and glass, then immerse it in another medium.
  • Images in two inclined mirrors — Place an object between two plane mirrors and change the angle. Count the images.
  • Guiding light in an optical fibre — Launch a ray inside a glass core and check whether the surrounding layer keeps it trapped.
  • Prism: angle of minimum deviation — Change the angle of incidence on a glass prism and measure the angle of emergence and the deviation.
  • Dispersion through a prism — Send white light through glass and separate its component colours.
  • Refraction and total internal reflection — Send a ray across a boundary between two optical media.
  • Spherical mirrors: ray diagram — Concave and convex spherical mirrors: move the object - even to infinity - and see where the image forms and…
  • Total internal reflection — Shine a laser from glass towards air. Beyond the critical angle no light escapes.
  • Brewster’s angle — Reflect unpolarised light from glass. At Brewster’s angle the reflected beam is completely polarised.
  • Single slit diffraction — Narrow a slit and observe its diffraction pattern spread.
  • Diffraction grating orders — Pass monochromatic light through many equally spaced slits and locate the bright orders.
  • Double slit interference — Pass light through two slits and watch bright and dark fringes form.
  • Fringe width in Young’s experiment — Change the geometry and see the bright-fringe spacing respond.
  • Polarisation and Malus’s law — Rotate an analyser after a polariser and watch the transmitted light fade.
  • Resolving two stars (Rayleigh criterion) — Two point sources make overlapping diffraction patterns. Are they resolved by this aperture?
  • Thin-film interference — Reflect light from the two faces of a thin film and tune its thickness through bright and dark conditions.
